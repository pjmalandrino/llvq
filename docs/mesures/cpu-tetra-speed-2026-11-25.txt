THE CPU MATVECS ON RANDOM CODES AT THE 4B'S SHAPES: TETRA 1.56x AND INT4 G128 1.38x ON ONE NEON CORE, SAME SUMS (2026-11-25)
=============================================================================================================================
Why:    the CPU backend (docs/mesures/cpu-tetra-2026-11-07.txt) served the 4B at 8.65 tok/s on 12 threads. Reading
        where a token's time goes came first; this journal holds the microbenchmark that answered it and the two
        kernel changes it pointed to.
Cost:   $0. Apple M3 Max (12 P + 4 E cores), macOS, rustc 1.95.0, profile `ci` (release optimisation, no LTO),
        under ~/.llvq-guard/heavy.sh (nice 15), the machine shared with other sessions (load 3.5 to 6.6).
Object: no model. Random 47-bit Tetra labels and gain bits through the served transcode (tetra48), random int4
        records, at the served 4B's sites. No file was read.
Code:   tests/cpu_tetra.rs `bench_cpu_matvecs_at_the_4b_shapes` (ignored), branch cpu/compat-and-speed-2026-11-25:
          LLVQ_THREADS=<n> cargo test --profile ci -p llvq-llm --test cpu_tetra -- --ignored --nocapture bench_
Nature: every time *measured*: the median of 7 rounds of about 0.15 s, the range of the rounds in brackets; GB/s and
        the per-token sum *computed* from them. NEON only: AVX2 and AVX-512 are not measured anywhere.

WHERE A TOKEN'S TIME GOES (before, 8 threads)
---------------------------------------------
The served 4B per token: Tetra q, k, gate, up on 36 layers and down on 24; int4 g128 v, o on 36 and down on 12; the
tied int4 g64 head once. The sum of the rows below is 105.4 ms (computed), against 115 ms a token end to end at 12
threads in the 2026-11-07 journal: the matvecs are the token, and the Tetra decode is 83 % of them. The candle
adapter (rotation, trio order, tensor copies) costs 17.6 us a prepare and no time measurable on a matvec: the
slices arm and the tensor arm agree at one thread (3.18 against 3.17 ms) and swap places at eight, where rounds are
bimodal on a shared machine.

WHAT CHANGED
------------
  1. NEON: the two candidates of a table row load as one 128-bit vector, the code byte's mask as one 64-bit vector,
     and a bit select merges them; before, three scalar merges and three general-to-vector moves per block. Most of
     the gain.
  2. Fixed-size tables with masked indices and the row's words read two at a time from the three u32 they fill: no
     bounds check in the block loop. gscale[g] / |y| from a 64-entry table formed per matrix call.
  3. int4: one SIMD call per row, four accumulators inside a group, the scaled group sums kept in lanes.
The block order of every sum is unchanged, so a batch and a single row round alike; a two-accumulator variant broke
the 1e-5 batch test and was dropped.

WHAT THIS DOES NOT SHOW
-----------------------
  - No token was generated: the end-to-end rate after the change is not measured.
  - AVX2 takes changes 2 and 3; it is type-checked for x86_64 Linux and runs in CI, never timed. Change 1 has no AVX2
    counterpart yet. No AVX-512 path.
  - One machine, shared, one run per arm.

THE AVX2 PATH, REWORKED FROM ITS ASSEMBLY (2026-11-25, NIGHT)
------------------------------------------------------------
Why:    on 8 vCPU of an AMD EPYC 7R13 the AVX2 Tetra matvec was 6.4 to 8.6 times slower than NEON on an M3 core,
        where int4 was about 3 times slower: the AVX2 Tetra decode carried its own factor of 2 to 3.
Cost:   $0. No x86 CPU was used. Branch cpu/avx2-tetra-2026-11-25, three commits on nuit/runtime-cpu-2026-11-25.
Nature: the instruction counts are *measured* in the emitted x86_64 assembly (rustc 1.95.0, opt-level 3). Every
        time below is *measured under Rosetta 2 on the M3 Max, not on an x86 CPU*: a translated binary, useful as
        a before/after signal only. Rosetta reports AVX2, FMA and F16C only with ROSETTA_ADVERTISE_AVX=1; without
        it the bench ran the scalar path.

What the assembly showed (avx2::row, two blocks per iteration of for_each_word):
  - Everything was inlined and VEX-encoded: no out-of-line intrinsic, no SSE code, no vzeroupper in the loop.
  - About 115 instructions a block. About 65 were scalar: Tables::locate (about 45 integer operations), then the
    candidate merge as xor/and/xor on general registers and one general-to-vector move per section.
  - The block norm was an f32 horizontal sum and a saturating float-to-int with two compares and a branch.
  - avx2::rows called its block body as an out-of-line closure, once per block (for_each_word has three call
    sites); the closure is AVX2 code and ended with a vzeroupper.
  - int4: half's to_f32 on x86_64 is a runtime F16C check and an out-of-line call, twice per group. The Tetra
    row tail called fmaf for each mul_add, the crate not being built with the fma feature.
  - No gathers, no store-forwarding pattern in the loop.

What changed, in commit order (each bit-identical on the AVX2 path: a fingerprint of the outputs of 6 Tetra shapes
x 5 batch sizes and 3 int4 shapes, taken before the first change, is unchanged after each):
  3336c4a  candidates selected by one byte shuffle of the 16-byte entry (a 4 KiB x86-only table); the norm from
           the selected bytes in integers (squares by |y| <= 11, asserted at build, then psadbw); the candidate
           table aligned to 64 bytes; the batch body inlined. About 97 instructions a block.
  d47526c  binary16 by vcvtph2ps in the int4 row and in an AVX2 Tetra tail; Isa::detect requires F16C.
  tip      the addresses of eight blocks formed at once in AVX2 (32-bit lanes, variable shifts) and stored a
           chunk of 64 blocks ahead of the decode, which reads them back as loads. About 54 instructions a block
           in the decode and 9.5 in the vector preparation, of which about 12 scalar.

The tip is SLOWER than d47526c under Rosetta, by 1.75x on gate/up, while it runs about a third fewer x86
instructions a block. Two probes of the tip on the same machine: the decode through the staged addresses with
the scalar locate in place of the vector preparation read 13.66 ms on gate/up; with the vector preparation,
16.08 ms. Rosetta has no 256-bit registers and translates the variable shifts and cross-lane inserts into several
NEON operations each, so this says how Rosetta runs the vector preparation, not how a Zen 3 core does. Which of
d47526c and the tip is faster on x86 is open: only the timed job on an x86 CPU decides it.

WHAT THIS DOES NOT SHOW
  - No x86 CPU was timed. The x86 job must time d47526c and the tip side by side.
  - Thread scaling was not touched: the row split is unchanged.
