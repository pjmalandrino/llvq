\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Models and encoding.} Qwen3-4B, 8B and 14B~\citep{qwen3_2025} are
each encoded with the first 131{,}072 tokens of DCLM-Edu~\citep{dclmedu}, the
same text at every size, then trained and assembled as in \S\ref{sec:model}.

\paragraph{Quality.} The benchmark is MMLU~\citep{mmlu2021}, 5-shot,
micro-averaged over all 14{,}042 questions of its test split, with the answer
read from the logits of the four answer letters. Each file is scored on its
dense reconstruction in an ordinary forward pass. The kernel computes with the
same decoded weights, and the checks of \S\ref{sec:kernel} tie the two. At 4B,
we scored the file one step before sealing, with the same int4 matrices
rebuilt at load by the same quantizer. On 57 questions, the sealed 4B file
gives the same answers and logits. Our gaps to FP16 and AWQ, and between our
own files, are paired on the same questions, with a 95\,\% interval from a
bootstrap that resamples questions within each subject (10{,}000 draws) and an
exact McNemar test. On the full test set these intervals are 0.7 to 1.7 points
wide, so how small a gap this test separates depends on the pair. A gap whose interval contains zero is \emph{not
separated}. We ran no equivalence test, so this does not mean the two scores
are equal.

\paragraph{Perplexity.} We also read each file's perplexity on the raw test
split of WikiText-2~\citep{wikitext2016}, over 12 non-overlapping windows of
4{,}096 tokens, on the dense reconstruction in f16, as for MMLU, on the L40S.
Each value is one run. Two files read on the same windows give per-window
losses that pair, and we give such pairs a 95\,\% $t$ interval over the 12
windows.

\paragraph{Reasoning.} To score generated reasoning, not one logit per
question, we add all 1{,}319 test problems of GSM8K~\citep{gsm8k2021},
zero-shot in the model's chat template with its thinking block left empty
(Qwen3's non-thinking mode~\citep{qwen3_2025}). The prompt asks for the answer
in a \texttt{\textbackslash{}boxed\{\}}. Decoding is greedy and stops at the
end of the turn or after 1{,}024 tokens. The answer is the last number in the
last box, or the last number of the text when no box closes. Unlike MMLU, our
files are scored through the kernel, on the per-matrix path. FP16 and AWQ generate in vLLM from
the same prompt tokens, and one grader scores every arm. Gaps are paired, with
a 95\,\% normal interval and an exact McNemar test.

\paragraph{Scores and the served engine.} Every score in this paper was
measured with the expanded attention (\S\ref{sec:4e}). The served engine
differs from that form only by its attention, which a perplexity tolerance
ties to the expanded one, and by the persistent kernel, which returns the per-matrix
tokens. As a direct check, 50 GSM8K problems per size ran through the
persistent kernel: 40, 46 and 46 right at 4B, 8B and 14B, the counts of the
earlier engine on those problems.

\paragraph{Speed and memory.} Decode speed is measured on one NVIDIA L40S at
batch 1 with greedy sampling. Our files run in our engine under their served
configuration (\S\ref{sec:4e}) and generate 256 tokens after a 5-token prompt.
Their speed is tokens over wall-clock time, prompt included. FP16 and AWQ run
in vLLM 0.26.0~\citep{vllm2023}, AWQ with the Marlin kernel, and generate 128
tokens, timed the same way. Both engines report the median of five timed
rounds, after one warm-up round in ours and two in vLLM, and the five rounds
of every median lie within 2\,\% of each other. IQ2\_S runs in
llama.cpp~\citep{llamacpp} at commit \texttt{cd959bae9}, built for the card,
with \texttt{llama-bench}: 256 tokens after a prompt of 5 and of 8{,}192
tokens, as the median of five rounds. Its speed leaves the prompt out.
IQ2\_XXS at 4B keeps its earlier measurement, with build b10689 on 128 tokens
from an empty prompt, as the mean of five repetitions. The memory of
Table~\ref{tab:main} counts only weight bytes (\S\ref{sec:file}): GPU buffers
for our files, weight files for the others. Card memory
(\S\ref{sec:duel}, \S\ref{sec:context}) is read from the driver every 100~ms,
as the peak minus the idle level.

\paragraph{Baselines.} FP16 is Qwen's published checkpoint, stored in bf16 and
run in f16. AWQ is Qwen's official w4g128 checkpoint at each size, scored on
MMLU in our harness after conversion to f16 and timed in vLLM on the packed
checkpoint. IQ2\_S is the llama.cpp format nearest our bits per parameter. A
preregistered rule picked it from the file sizes, before any score. We built
it with \texttt{llama-quantize} and an importance matrix from the DCLM-Edu
text of our files. Its perplexity is read on our 12 windows. As a gate, FP16
through llama.cpp reads within 0.05\,\% of FP16 in our engine at every size.
Its MMLU and GSM8K go through \texttt{llama-server} with our prompts and our
grader. IQ2\_XXS at 4B is an earlier build, with an importance matrix from
131{,}072 tokens of C4, scored the same way. We cite the
MMLU of QTIP, QuIP\# and the original LLVQ from \citet{llvq2026} and do not
run them.

\paragraph{Quality.} Our files lose quality to both baselines, and the loss
shrinks as the model grows (Table~\ref{tab:gaps}). On the same questions, the
gap to FP16 falls from 6.77 MMLU points at 4B to 3.22 at 14B, and the gap to
AWQ from 4.76 to 2.46. The trend is not established at every step: the
0.56-point fall of the gap to AWQ from 4B to 8B is not separated from zero
($z=1.1$). AWQ itself sits 2.00 $[1.50, 2.51]$, 1.27 $[0.83, 1.70]$ and 0.75
$[0.40, 1.12]$ points below FP16.

\paragraph{Perplexity.} Perplexity ranks the files differently from MMLU. It
puts our 4B 6.9\,\% below AWQ, where MMLU puts it behind, and it finds the gap
to AWQ smallest at 4B, where MMLU finds it smallest at 14B
(Table~\ref{tab:gaps}). Against FP16 our files read 2.8 to 8.0\,\% higher. All
six gaps are separated from zero, paired over the 12 windows. AWQ's own
perplexity is 10.5\,\% above FP16's at 4B.

\paragraph{Memory.} Our files need about half of AWQ's bits per parameter, 45
to 52\,\%, and part of that comes from the embedding tables. Ours are in 4
bits, while AWQ keeps its embedding and head in f16. These tables weigh most
at 8B (\S\ref{sec:q4emb}), which is where AWQ needs the most bits per
parameter. If AWQ stored them in 4 bits like ours, our files would need 64 to
65\,\% of its bits per parameter. We computed this from the bytes and did not
measure AWQ's quality with such tables.

\paragraph{Against other 2-bit formats.} IQ2\_S, the llama.cpp format nearest
our bits, scores below our files on MMLU and perplexity at every size and
decodes faster; \S\ref{sec:duel} gives that comparison. At 4B, IQ2\_XXS scores
23.6 points below our file for 0.25 fewer bits per parameter. The cited rows
of Table~\ref{tab:main} come from another harness and count bits per quantized
weight, so they place our 63.37 only roughly and do not rank it. It is close
to the 62.8 of the original LLVQ with fine-tuning, and above the 59.5 of QTIP
with its 3INST code.

\paragraph{Speed.} One launch per token makes the files 1.4 to 1.7 times
faster than the per-matrix path, for the same tokens
(Figure~\ref{fig:speed}). The sealed files decode 221.5, 142.5 and 86.3 tokens
per second, against 129.0, 103.0 and 62.8 for the per-matrix path under the
same configuration. Formed round by round in one job, the ratio is 1.717
$[1.714, 1.722]$ at 4B, 1.384 $[1.382, 1.385]$ at 8B and 1.375
$[1.369, 1.376]$ at 14B. With the expanded attention, our first
configuration, the per-matrix path reads 113.8, 95.0 and 57.2
(\S\ref{sec:4e}). After loading, the card holds 1.98, 3.36 and 5.64~GB, which
is the weights plus the CUDA context and the persistent kernel's buffers.

The 4-bit output head explains a large part of the files' lead over their own
dense reconstruction. In the first configuration, with the embedding and head
in f16 on both sides, the files decoded 55.0, 38.9 and 27.3 tokens per second
against 43.3, 26.4 and 16.9 for the dense arm of the same runs: 1.27, 1.47 and
1.61 times, from unrounded medians.

We form no ratio between our speeds and those of vLLM or llama.cpp, because
such a ratio would mix the format with the engine. vLLM, for one, runs FP16
faster than our dense path does. In levels, our files now decode above AWQ in
vLLM at every size, and below IQ2\_S in llama.cpp.

On generated reasoning, our files lose to both baselines at every size. All
six gaps are separated from zero, with every $p$ below $10^{-5}$
(Table~\ref{tab:gsm8k}). AWQ is harder to tell from FP16 on this test: it sits
3.11 points below at 4B, then 0.30 and $-0.08$ at 8B and 14B, which the test
set cannot separate from zero.

Reasoning costs our files more than multiple choice does, although the two
ways of counting disagree on where. In points, the loss is larger on GSM8K
only at 4B, 9.63 against 6.77, where the GSM8K interval excludes the MMLU gap.
At 8B and 14B the intervals contain the MMLU gaps of 5.48 and 3.22. In errors,
the sealed files make 2.22, 1.69 and 1.69 times as many as FP16 on GSM8K,
against 1.23, 1.22 and 1.15 times on MMLU, so the cost is higher at every
size.

\paragraph{Two engines.} The engine does not explain these gaps. We ran the
FP16 4B checkpoint through our dense path, and it scored 91.51 against 92.12
in vLLM, $-0.61$ points $[-1.27, 0.06]$, $p=0.12$. Against this same-engine
FP16, the 4B file loses 9.02 points $[7.07, 10.97]$, against 9.63 across
engines. On the 50 problems of a pilot, the kernel on the L40S and the dense
reconstruction on an Apple GPU give the same 50 answers for the 4B file.

IQ2\_S is what a llama.cpp user would pick at our memory, so we compared it
with our files on the same card, the same day, under one preregistration
(Table~\ref{tab:duel}, Figure~\ref{fig:frontier}). The result is a trade: our
files score higher, and llama.cpp runs faster. Bits per parameter are counted
here from file bytes on both sides. That puts our files 0.03 to 0.09 above
the served count of Table~\ref{tab:main}, because the file also carries the
tokenizer and stores the tail and the row scales in wider types
(Table~\ref{tab:files}).

\paragraph{Quality.} Our files win perplexity and MMLU at every size, by a
margin that shrinks as the model grows (Table~\ref{tab:duelgaps}). The MMLU
lead goes from 9.08 points at 4B to 2.89 at 14B. On GSM8K our files lead at 4B
and 8B, but at 14B the two are not separated, where we had predicted a win.
Two caveats apply. The 14B IQ2\_S file is 0.07 bits per parameter smaller than
ours. And our scores were not measured again that day: they are those of
Tables~\ref{tab:main} and~\ref{tab:gsm8k}.

\paragraph{Speed.} llama.cpp decodes faster at every size and both prompt
lengths. After a 5-token prompt it leads by 60.1, 63.9 and 33.5 tokens per
second at 4B, 8B and 14B, and after 8{,}192 tokens by 34.6, 44.6 and 28.0.
Because the engines differ we form no ratio, but the order is the same in
bytes of weights read per second (Table~\ref{tab:duel}). The speeds of that
table are the decode alone, so ours sit 1.4 to 4.3\,\% above those of
Table~\ref{tab:main}, which include the prompt.

\paragraph{Memory after a short prompt.} After a short prompt the two hold
about as much memory, once llama.cpp's host memory is counted. The card peaks
lie within about 0.3~GB of each other at every size (Table~\ref{tab:duel}).
llama.cpp also keeps its input embedding in host memory, 0.27, 0.27 and
0.33~GB that the card does not see. With it, IQ2\_S holds 2.43, 3.62 and
5.73~GB: more than our files at 4B and 8B, and as much at 14B. Long prompts
change this picture (\S\ref{sec:context}).

\paragraph{One rung below.} A llama.cpp file one rung below ours in size loses
much more quality. UD-IQ2\_XXS holds 0.2 to 0.3 fewer bits per parameter than
our files and scores 27.34, 8.62 and 7.88 MMLU points below them. At 4B it
loops under greedy decoding: 999 of its 1{,}319 GSM8K answers reach the token
limit, so its score of 9.17 measures the loop.

\paragraph{Decode speed.} A long prompt slows the decode by 18 to 29\,\%. From
a 5-token prompt to one of 8{,}192 tokens, our files go from 226.2 to 161.5
tokens per second at 4B, from 144.5 to 110.0 at 8B and from 87.3 to 71.5 at
14B (Table~\ref{tab:context}, Figure~\ref{fig:context}, left). IQ2\_S stays
ahead at 8{,}192 tokens (Table~\ref{tab:duel}).

\paragraph{Time to the first token.} The per-matrix prefill makes a long
prompt wait, and the chain prefill removes most of that wait at 4B. After
8{,}192 prompt tokens, the per-matrix prefill delivers the first token after
35.05~s at 4B, 46.30~s at 8B and 80.74~s at 14B. Timed with it in one job, the
chain prefill (\S\ref{sec:4e}) brings the 4B from 37.22~s to 6.74~s
(Table~\ref{tab:context}, Figure~\ref{fig:context}, right). The 256 tokens
that follow are the same after either prefill, and on 200 GSM8K problems both
give 165 right answers with no discordant pair. The 8B and the 14B still use
the per-matrix prefill. We did not measure llama.cpp's time to the first
token.

\paragraph{Card memory.} After a long prompt our files hold more card memory
than IQ2\_S (Figure~\ref{fig:peak}). On the day of the comparison they peaked
at about three times what we had predicted, because the prefill's attention
kept three score tensors alive at once. Keeping one, with the same logits bit
for bit, brings the peak after 8{,}192 tokens to 5.95, 7.39 and 10.54~GB.
IQ2\_S peaks at 3.52, 4.71 and 6.90~GB, still 2.43, 2.68 and 3.64~GB lower. At
4B the chain prefill peaks at 3.63~GB, read in its own job.

\paragraph{Several requests.} With concurrent requests our engine falls behind
AWQ in vLLM. A batched form of the kernel decodes requests that arrive
together, while a lone request stays on the persistent kernel. From 1 to 128
concurrent requests, the 4B goes from 221.9 to 3{,}067.5 tokens per second in
aggregate, and every answer equals its batch-1 answer. AWQ in vLLM goes from
203.0 to 10{,}146.2. The batched engine also allocates its KV caches at load,
19.3~GB at 4B for 64 slots of 2{,}048 positions, and it is not the default.

Both steps of the recipe gain MMLU at every size, and all six gains are
separated from zero (Table~\ref{tab:chain}).

\paragraph{Perplexity does not follow the last step.} The last step, int4
projections and 4-bit tables, raises the perplexity of the 4B and the 8B, on
the windows of \S\ref{sec:setup}. It goes from 12.33
to 12.58 at 4B, $+2.1\,\%$ $[1.5, 2.6]$, and from 9.57 to 9.71 at 8B,
$+1.4\,\%$ $[1.1, 1.7]$, worse on all 12 windows at both sizes. At 14B it goes
from 8.46 to 8.44, $-0.2\,\%$ $[-0.9, 0.5]$, not separated. So the step gains
MMLU at every size and costs perplexity at the two smaller ones. We did not
measure which half of the step costs perplexity, the 4-bit tables or the
projections moved to int4. Data: paper2-ppl-sealing.csv.

\paragraph{Which projections get the int4 bytes.} At 8B the embedding tables
take the largest share of the parameters (\S\ref{sec:q4emb}), so at about 2.7
bits per parameter int4 gets fewer projections than at 4B and 14B
(Table~\ref{tab:files}). Our 8B file, with int4 on \texttt{down\_proj} of
layers 10 to 26 and not on \texttt{o\_proj}, scores 0.77 points $[0.26, 1.28]$
above int4 on all of \texttt{o\_proj} plus \texttt{down\_proj} of layers 15
to 20, which scores 68.81 at 2.7047 bits per parameter ($p=0.004$). We had predicted the opposite order. The 4B and
14B files keep \texttt{o\_proj} in int4, and we have not tested the 8B choice
there.

\paragraph{Selection on the test set.} These choices were made on MMLU test
questions, with no held-out split. At 8B we kept the better of the two files
above, under a preregistered rule. At 4B the window of layers 12 to 23 was the
best of three windows of twelve layers, scored on the full test set with an
earlier 4B file. The projection types sent to int4 were ranked at 4B on
samples of 2{,}280 questions. The 14B window follows a rule fixed before
measuring. It is the largest centred window that the bytes freed by the
tables pay for, as at 4B. So the MMLU scores of our files carry a
selection bias that we did not measure. For \texttt{o\_proj},
the one choice checked on questions that did not select it, the gain is
$+3.12$ points on the 2{,}280 that selected it and $+1.55$ on the other
11{,}762. GSM8K and perplexity chose nothing. We also tried a criterion that reads no
benchmark, the ratio of the Hessian-weighted errors of \Tetra{} and int4 per
matrix. On an earlier 4B file it did not rank six of the seven projection types
(\texttt{v\_proj} was already int4) as MMLU does (Spearman $\rho = 0.54$,
$p = 0.30$, an exploratory reading), so it chose nothing here.
