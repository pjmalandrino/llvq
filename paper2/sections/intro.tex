\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A 14-billion-parameter model in FP16 needs 29.5~GB for its weights alone, more
than any single consumer GPU holds. At batch 1, generating a token also means
reading every weight once, so the same bytes set both the memory footprint and
the speed. Bringing weights down to two or three bits per parameter is what
lets such a model run on local hardware.

At two bits per weight, vector quantization on the Leech lattice $\Lambda_{24}$
gives the best quality in the comparison of \citet{llvq2026}, ahead of
QuIP\#~\citep{quipsharp2024} and QTIP~\citep{qtip2024}. It encodes blocks of
24 weights with 48 bits each. Its codebook, however, has $1.1\times10^{14}$
points. Fast 2-bit kernels decode their codes with a small lookup table, and a
table that size does not exist. In earlier work~\citep{llvq1preprint} we served
this code anyway, by unfolding it at load time into a wider layout that a
kernel reads with shifts and masks. It worked, but the kernel read 4.804 bits
per weight from VRAM for 2 bits of code, so most of the saving was lost on the
GPU (Figure~\ref{fig:gap}).

This paper removes that loss, then builds complete models around the result.

The first part is a new codebook on the same lattice, which we call \Tetra{}.
Reading the 24 coordinates in three blocks of eight turns the Golay code inside
$\Lambda_{24}$ into a trellis with 64 states per cut. A lattice point is then
addressed by an 8-bit state and three 11-bit rows of one 16~KiB table. The
block is still 48 bits. The kernel decodes it with six table loads and reads
2.148 bits per weight.

The second part turns the codebook into models worth serving. The lattice codes
alone leave Qwen3-4B about 15 MMLU points below FP16. Three changes close part
of that gap without touching a single code: training the per-row scales against
the FP16 model, storing the projections that lose the most in 4-bit integers,
and storing the embedding tables in 4 bits to pay for them. The files stay near
2.7 bits per parameter over the whole model.

Our contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \Tetra{}, a codebook on $\Lambda_{24}$ whose addresses factor through a
  64-state trellis of the Golay code and one 16~KiB table, with the truncation
  rule this forces and an estimate of what it costs (\S\ref{sec:tetra}).
\item A fused kernel that decodes it inside the matrix-vector product and reads
  2.148 bits per weight, against 4.804 for our earlier layout and 4.179 for
  4-bit AWQ (\S\ref{sec:kernel}).
\item A recipe for complete files at about 2.7 bits per parameter, measured
  step by step on Qwen3-4B, 8B and 14B on the full MMLU test set, with paired
  confidence intervals (\S\ref{sec:model}, \S\ref{sec:chain}).
\item Quality, speed and memory of the served files against FP16 and AWQ at the
  three sizes, and against 2-bit formats at 4B (\S\ref{sec:main}).
\end{itemize}

The files do not match AWQ on quality. They score 4.76, 4.21 and 2.46 points
below it at 4B, 8B and 14B, with the gap shrinking as the model grows. They use
about half its memory: 2.7 bits per parameter against 5.3 to 6.0. Served in
our engine they decode 113.8, 95.0 and 57.2 tokens per second at 4B, 8B and
14B, in 1.38, 2.76 and 5.04~GB of weights. At 4B they
score 23.6 points above llama.cpp's IQ2\_XXS, which uses 2.48 bits per
parameter. Everything was
measured on one card. The campaigns behind the main results were preregistered
with predictions, and we report the ones that were wrong
(Appendix~\ref{app:record}).
