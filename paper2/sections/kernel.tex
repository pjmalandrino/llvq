\section{The kernel}
\label{sec:kernel}

\subsection{Decoding inside the matrix-vector product}

The kernel keeps the thread layout of our earlier one~\citep{llvq1preprint}.
One warp (threads that run in lockstep) computes each output row, and a
256-thread block holds 8 rows. Each thread of the warp, or lane, decodes one
block of weights at a time. The activation goes into shared memory in tiles of
$T$ blocks ($96T$ bytes), with two barriers per tile. A shuffle reduction sums
the lanes' results, and a last step multiplies by $\sigma_{\mathrm{row}}$ and
adds the unquantized tail. Only the block decode differs.

Decoding a block takes six table loads and two small lookups, for the gain and
the norm. Three choices make it fast. First, each coordinate value is held in a
register table as a biased byte, $\mathrm{val}+128$. Placed in the low byte of
\texttt{0x4b0000\_\_}, it reads exactly as the float $2^{23}+\mathrm{byte}$,
and one subtraction gives the value back. A coordinate thus costs one byte
permute, one add and one fused multiply-add, with no integer-to-float
conversion. Second, every loop is unrolled with constant indices, and the
compiler reports 40 registers and no spill to local memory for the benchmark kernel, as
for \Planes{}. Third, no branch depends on the data, so lanes never diverge.

Counted from the source, a block's rank decode and 24 multiply-adds take about
150 instructions (about 60 on the FMA pipe, about 85 on the ALU pipe and six
loads), and the gain and the norm about 20 more. A decoder that extracts and
converts each coordinate needs about 380, plus 24 conversions.

The kernel reads the 48 bits the file stores for each block, and no code is
expanded. Loading only reorders the bits of each word and pads each row to a
multiple of 8~bytes. In the same process, the sealed 4B file loads in 4.1~s
and its dense reconstruction (the file decoded to f16) in 72.9~s.

\paragraph{The rest of a served model.} The int4 projections use a separate
kernel with the same thread layout. It dequantizes each weight with its group's
scale and offset and accumulates in f32. It stages the whole activation in
shared memory. A 14B \texttt{down\_proj} needs 69{,}632~B, more than the
48~KiB default, so the host opts in to that amount, under the L40S limit of
101{,}376~B~\citep{cudaguide}. Two small kernels serve the 4-bit embedding
tables, and a third rotates the activations online, 96 times a token at 4B,
once per group of projections that share a rotation.

\paragraph{How the kernel is checked.} Before any timing, we recompute in f64,
from the same decoded weights, every output row of the 4B shapes in every
benchmark arm that reads weights: 1{,}105{,}920 rows per arm.
Each must match the kernel's row within $10^{-5}\cdot\sum|w\cdot x|$, or
$10^{-3}$ for AWQ and cuBLAS, which write f16. The worst \Tetra{} row is off by
$2.0\times10^{-8}\cdot\sum|w\cdot x|$. The decoder's CUDA source, compiled for
the CPU, gives the same 24 lattice coordinates as an independent Rust decoder,
bit for bit, on 4{,}000 test words: the origin and the all-ones word, each
with and without the gain bit, and random words.

End to end, the kernel and the dense reconstruction agree until two logits
nearly tie. On one prompt and in one process, they generate the same 256
greedy tokens at 4B and 8B for each sealed file. At both sizes the text falls into a
one-sentence loop. At 14B they first differ at token 78, and at token 137 when
both run with f16 embedding tables. A second check runs 203 prompt tokens
through the kernel, in one call and in 203 single-token calls. Both give the
same next token at all three sizes. The two paths still move a logit by up to
0.095, 1.35 and 0.79 at 4B, 8B and 14B, for logits up to 30.4, 36.6 and 27.5.
The divergence at token 78 is consistent with a near tie between two logits,
flipped by differences of this size, but we did not measure the margin there.
Our preregistration counts a first divergence at or after token 32 as not a
defect.

\paragraph{The activation tile.}\label{sec:tile} The size of the activation
tile matters to \Tetra{} and hardly to the other arms. The tile $T$ changes
no output bit and costs no storage. On an Ada GPU (sm\_89), it sets how much of each
SM's 128~KiB pool of L1 and shared memory is left to cache the decoder
tables~\citep{cudaguide}. Occupancy does not move. Six 256-thread blocks fit at
every tile, filling the 1{,}536 threads an SM holds, and shared memory never
binds. At $T = 128$, 64 and 32, one process per tile on one L40S, the median
pass takes 2.281, 2.291 and 2.392~ms with no weights, 5.068, 5.011 and
5.047~ms for \Planes{}, and 4.353, 3.701 and 3.841~ms for \Tetra{} (data:
tuile-l40s.csv). Each arm's range, largest over smallest, is 4.9, 1.1 and
17.6\,\%. The three arms share the thread layout, the activation copy, the
barriers and the occupancy. \Planes{} reads one entry per block from a 12~KiB
table, \Tetra{} seven from 18.4~KiB. We attribute \Tetra{}'s larger range to
pressure on L1 but have not proven it, because our rented platform exposes no
performance counters. We serve $T=64$ on sm\_89. On an sm\_120 card, an
earlier sweep over the same 252 matrices puts \Tetra{} fastest at $T=32$ and
\Planes{} at $T=128$, with \Tetra{} at 0.82 to 1.03 times the speed of
\Planes{} depending on the tile. On an A100 (\S\ref{sec:a100}), \Tetra{} is
fastest at $T=128$.

The served engine runs a whole token, every layer and the output head, in one
cooperative launch (Figure~\ref{fig:4e}). The kernel above is launched once
per matrix, so it leaves the card between any two operations of a token: 252
projections at 4B, plus the norms, the rotations and the attention. Our engine
first served the files that way.

The grid has one 512-thread block per SM, 142 on the L40S. A token is a fixed
sequence of phases, and between two phases every block waits at a grid-wide
barrier, 218 times a token at 4B. Inside a phase the blocks claim output rows
from a shared counter. A \Tetra{} row is computed as in the per-matrix
kernel: the same lanes, the same block order, the same 24
products in the same order and the same last step. In the three places where
a compiler could fuse a multiply with an add, the operations are written out
by hand. The int4 rows read the same words, laid out once at load so that the
four words a lane needs from a tile sit side by side and one 128-bit load
fetches them. The card holds those bytes once.

\paragraph{Same tokens.} The persistent kernel must return the tokens of the
per-matrix path, and each load checks it on the card before serving. In the
runs behind our speeds it returned the same 256 greedy tokens at 4B, 8B and
14B, after prompts of 5, 2{,}048, 4{,}096 and 8{,}192 tokens.

\paragraph{Attention.}\label{sec:attn} Qwen3 shares each key and value head
among several query heads. The \emph{expanded} form copies each cached head
to its query heads before the product, and every score in this paper ran
under it. The served engine reads each cached head once, in an attention
kernel of its own. Its sums run in another order, so its logits are not bit
for bit those of the expanded form. We serve it under a tolerance fixed in
advance: paired WikiText-2 perplexity within $\pm0.12\,\%$ of the reference
form, which is what this paired test resolves on one file. Reading each head
once moves perplexity by $+0.0095$, $-0.0048$ and $+0.0023\,\%$ at 4B, 8B and
14B. The attention kernel moves it by a further $+0.0018$, $+0.0043$ and
$-0.0108\,\%$, over 12 windows at 4B and 6 at 8B and 14B. Every interval lies
inside the band.

\paragraph{Prompts.} The per-matrix path prefills a prompt in chunks of
4{,}096 positions. At 4B the served engine instead feeds the prompt through a
multi-row pass of the persistent kernel, the \emph{chain prefill}. It reads
the served words in place, and its paired perplexity is $+0.0007\,\%$ from the
per-matrix prefill's. That pass does not yet carry the 8B and the 14B
(\S\ref{sec:context}).

\paragraph{Limits.} The persistent kernel decodes one request at a time, with
greedy sampling, on the three sealed shapes. It exists for NVIDIA cards only
and is measured on the L40S only.
