\section{The Tetra codebook}
\label{sec:tetra}

\subsection{The lattice and the quantizer}

We use the integer form of the Leech lattice, up to the overall scale that the
quantizer sets. A vector of 24 integers $y\in\mathbb{Z}^{24}$ lies in
$\Lambda_{24}$ when it can be written as
\begin{equation}
y_j = p + 2c_j + 4k_j,\qquad \textstyle\sum_j k_j \equiv p \pmod 2 .
\label{eq:leech}
\end{equation}
Here $p$ is a bit, the $k_j$ are integers, and $c$ is a codeword of the binary
Golay code $\mathcal{G}_{24}$. Two constraints pick the lattice out of
$\mathbb{Z}^{24}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:geom}a). First, $c$ must be one of the
$2^{12}$ Golay codewords among the $2^{24}$ bit patterns. Second, one parity
check ties all 24 coordinates together. A decoder has to respect both.

The encoder uses the shape-gain scheme of \citet{llvq2026}. It rotates the
input~\citep{quip2023,quarot2024} and runs a GPTQ-style loop~\citep{gptq2023}.
For each block of 24 rotated weights, it looks for the lattice point under a
length cap whose direction is closest to the block, and rebuilds the weights as
\begin{equation}
\hat{w}_j = \frac{y_j}{\lVert y\rVert}\,\mathrm{gain}[g]\,\sigma_{\mathrm{row}},
\qquad \lVert y\rVert=\sqrt{16m},
\label{eq:recon}
\end{equation}
where $m$ is the shell index. The bit $g$ selects the gain, and
$\sigma_{\mathrm{row}}$ is one scale per output row, stored in f64 and read by
the kernel in f32. We change two things:
the region the encoder searches (\S\ref{sec:kept}) and the bits it writes for
each block. The loop, the rotation, the gain values and the row scales stay as
they were. The \Tetra{} encoder does not search every point. At two trial
scales, it keeps a few candidates per section and joins the best over the
trellis. It still runs once, offline, on a CPU.

\subsection{Three octads and a trellis}

An octad is a Golay codeword with exactly eight ones. Three octads that share
no position cover all 24 coordinates. We fix one such triple and read the
coordinates octad by octad. Cutting after coordinates 8 and 16 splits the
block into three sections of eight coordinates, one per octad. These cuts turn
the code into a trellis (Figure~\ref{fig:geom}b), a layered graph whose paths
spell out bit patterns. The node a path crosses at a cut is its state. Each cut
has 64 states, and no split of $\mathcal{G}_{24}$ into three sections of eight
has fewer. The
trellis has $64\times2\times16\times2 = 4096 = |\mathcal{G}_{24}|$ paths, so
every codeword is one path and every path is a codeword. We check this count
when we build the tables.

\subsection{State, rank vectors, and one table for three sections}
\label{sec:state}

The 48-bit word of a block (\S\ref{sec:decode}) holds eight bits of state.
They are the trellis state $s_8$ (6 bits), the shared parity $p$, and $r$, the
parity of $\sum k_j$ over section 1. The state $s_8$ holds exactly
what section 2 needs to know about section 1. On top of the code's own state,
the lattice adds only two bits, $p$ and $r$. That gives $64\times4 = 256$
lattice states at a cut.

Each section has a $k$-parity, the parity of the sum of its $k_j$. Only
section 1's is stored, as $r$. Section 2's, $\delta$, is read from its row
index (explained below). Section 3's is
\begin{equation}
r_3 = p \oplus r \oplus \delta ,
\label{eq:xor}
\end{equation}
so the parity constraint of Equation~\eqref{eq:leech} costs one XOR and no bits.

A section's pattern byte is the eight bits of $c$ that fall in it. Once $p$
and this byte are known, each coordinate's remainder mod 4 is fixed,
$o_j = p + 2c_j$. Coordinate $j$ can then only take the values
$o_j + 4\mathbb{Z}$, spaced 4 apart. We store its \emph{rank}, its position in
that list counted outward from zero, instead of its value. For $o=0$ the
values are $0, +4, -4, +8, \dots$, for $o=2$ they are $+2, -2, +6, \dots$, for
$o=1$ $+1, -3, +5, \dots$, and for $o=3$ $-1, +3, -5, \dots$. A section is
then a rank vector $\rho \in \{0,\dots,7\}^8$, four bits per rank, which fits
in one 32-bit table row.

Two properties let a single table serve every section of every block. First,
let $\mathrm{cost}(\rho)=\sum_j(2\rho_j+1)^2$, a stand-in for squared
distance. Sorting rank vectors by this cost gives the same order for every $p$
and every pattern byte. ``Keep the 2{,}048 cheapest'' then means one list
instead of 256, one for each parity and each of the 128 pattern bytes a
section admits. Second,
$\mathrm{cls}(\rho)=\sum_j[\rho_j\in\{1,2,5,6\}] \bmod 2$ equals
the parity of $\sum_j k_j$ for every pattern. We sort the table by this
class, so a row's position gives its parity with no extra field.

The table holds 4{,}096 rows: the 2{,}048 cheapest rank vectors of class 0,
then the 2{,}048 cheapest of class 1. Eleven bits pick a row within one half.
The middle section reads the same rows through its own list, the 2{,}048
cheapest overall, whatever their class. That list is $N_0 = 1240$ rows of
class 0, then 808 of class 1. The middle index alone thus gives the class of
its row, the $\delta$ that Equation~\eqref{eq:xor} needs.

\subsection{The word and the decode}
\label{sec:decode}

Figure~\ref{fig:word} shows the resulting 48-bit word next to the \Planes{}
record it replaces. Each field can take a power-of-two number of values, so
any 48-bit value is a legal word and decodes to a point of $\Lambda_{24}$. A
benchmark can therefore stream random words and check every decode against a
reference. Figure~\ref{fig:decode} shows the decode: six loads give the 24
values, and one of them waits for another. The suffix read needs the state that
the branch read returns.

The tables take 18{,}816~B in total. The rank table holds 4{,}096 rows, or
16{,}384~B. The branch table holds 1{,}024 16-bit entries, or 2{,}048~B. The
prefix and suffix tables take 128~B each. The last table holds 32 floats, the
values $1/\sqrt{16m}$. Its entry 0 is 0, so the origin, a legal word, decodes
to zeros with no special case. Trying every codeword with both parities and
every allowed row in each section gives $m\le26$ and $\max|y_j|=10$.

\subsection{What the construction costs}
\label{sec:kept}

\Tetra{} is a new codebook on the same lattice, with a different set of points
from the earlier one. Keeping the 2{,}048 cheapest rank vectors limits each
section on its own. The encoder therefore searches a product of three
8-dimensional regions, one per section, where \Planes{} searched one
24-dimensional ball. This split makes the six-load decode possible, and it is
the price the format pays.

On 20{,}000 Gaussian blocks at 2 bits per weight, in one process and with the
same gain rule, \Tetra{} keeps 88.80\,\% retention and the \Planes{} codebook
91.98\,\%. That is 9.2\,\% more mean squared error for \Tetra{}. Retention is
the bit rate the theoretical limit would need to reach our error, as a
percentage of the bit rate we use. On
20{,}000 rotated weight blocks from the first layer of Qwen3-0.6B, the gap is
the same, 3.2 points. Part of it comes from the table: sorting rank vectors by
$\mathrm{cost}(\rho)$ instead of by exact squared distance loses 0.6 points of
retention on Gaussian blocks.

Geometry predicts about as much. The shaping gain measures how much the shape
of the searched region lowers the error, compared with a cube. Its best value
for a product of three 8-dimensional balls is 0.7292~dB, against 1.0958~dB for
one 24-dimensional ball. The difference, 0.3666~dB, means 8.81\,\% more mean
squared error for the three balls. Our regions are cut by rank and by the
trellis, so they are neither balls nor continuous.

The construction does keep the lattice. Every decoded point meets both
constraints of Figure~\ref{fig:geom}. Its pattern bytes form a Golay codeword
because they come from a trellis path, and Equation~\eqref{eq:xor} gives the
$\sum k$ parity. Three separate $E_8$ quantizers ($E_8$ is the best lattice
in 8 dimensions) would keep neither. The
lattice alone is worth $G(E_8)/G(\Lambda_{24}) = 0.071682/0.065771 =
1.0899$~\citep{conway1999sphere}. Here $G$ is the normalized second moment,
the mean squared error of a lattice used as a quantizer at a fixed density of
points. By this measure, $E_8$ has 9.0\,\% more mean squared error than
$\Lambda_{24}$.

On the same model and evaluation code, \Tetra{} alone (without the changes of
\S\ref{sec:model}) scores 2.11 MMLU points below \Planes{} on a
2{,}280-question sample of MMLU, $[-0.95, +5.17]$, $p=0.11$. Its perplexity is
4.64\,\% lower, which is better. This sample does not separate the two MMLU
scores.
Encoding the model with the \Planes{} codebook and three draws of calibration
text gives MMLU scores up to 5.83 points apart on the same sample, a standard
deviation of 2.92. The encoding pipeline had also changed in the month between
the two files. Re-encoding the first layer of the \Planes{} file (4.2 million
indices) with the newer code changes 87\,\% of them. So this comparison does not separate the codebook from
the calibration draw or from that drift.
