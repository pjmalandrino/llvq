\section{VRAM Layouts: Decoupling the Disk Rate from the Memory Rate}
\label{sec:layouts}

The rate that determines which models fit on a GPU is the rate of the
format the kernel actually reads; the artifact's on-disk rate is a
different quantity. This
section is an experimentally mapped scale of that trade-off: four byte
formats of ours read by five kernels (one format gets two decoders), two
deployed competitors at 4 and 2 bits, an FP16 control and a cuBLAS control
on that control, a floor kernel that reads no weights at all, one benchmark
harness, one byte-accounting convention.

\textbf{Methodology.} All arms run in a single process on an NVIDIA L40S,
interleaved within every round in a fixed dispatch order; 7 rounds, the first
2 discarded. Each reported speedup is the \emph{median of the per-round
ratios} with its range, never a quotient of two minima, which would compare
rounds that did not coexist. All kernels use zero bytes of local memory, and
every row of every arm is verified against f64 before timing
(\S\ref{sec:decoder}).

Byte accounting is the kernel's: what each arm streams per matrix (payload,
exception side-channels, full-precision tail columns, per-row scales). Three
asymmetries follow, uncorrected. Per-class tables are excluded, being
resident constants of a few kilobytes against gigabytes of stream, though
\textsc{Golay70} alone loads two of them. The FP16 control bills its tail at
f16 where our arms bill theirs at f32, and the 4-bit competitor bills no tail
at all: both understate our arms. Bandwidth is the bench's
convention, computed from each arm's fastest kept round; on medians every reported percentage is unchanged.

Adding an arm perturbs the others, so the run has two phases: nine arms,
then the same nine plus the tenth. Across phases the nine common ratios move
by at most 0.36\%, which sets the resolution of this table; every difference
argued from it stands at several times that grain. \emph{Every row comes
from that one run} --- adding the 2-bit competitor to the earlier seven-arm
table would have set a row from one process beside rows from another, the
comparison this methodology exists to forbid, so the whole table was
re-measured with every arm present. The incumbents reproduce within
dispersion ten days later (\textsc{Planes14} $2.15\times$ both times, hoisted
\textsc{Golay70} $1.77\times$ then $1.78\times$). Data:
\texttt{docs/data/echelle-formats.csv}; raw log
\texttt{docs/mesures/f2-p3-qtip-banc-2026-08-21.txt}.
$^{\dagger}$The bench prints per-round ratios against FP16, not against
another arm, so the one cross-arm ratio we state (\S\ref{sec:qtip}) is
formed from the two medians and its range bounded outward by both arms'
min--max; it is the only ratio in this paper not formed round by round, and
the widening is in the conservative direction.

\textbf{\textsc{Slot32}} stores the decoded fields at fixed offsets inside a
bit-packed, byte-aligned record whose width is $9 + g + 24L$ bits: a 9-bit
class identifier, $g=1$ gain bit, a 24-bit sign mask, and one 24-bit one-hot
mask per non-zero magnitude level naming which coordinates carry it. That is
130 bits at the widest class ($L=5$) and 106 at $L=4$. The kernel addresses a
record by its byte offset and reads a fixed five-word (20~B) window, an
alignment bound the format guarantees rather than checks. It is the simplest
divergence-free format and our first to beat FP16 ($1.89\times$), but at
5.510~b/weight (per the byte accounting above) it is \emph{more}
memory than a 4-bit format: fast and pointless at scale.

\textbf{\textsc{Planes14}} is the observation that the four one-hot level
masks $m_1..m_4$ waste bits: blocks carry between three and five magnitude
levels, so three binary bit planes name each coordinate's level where the
one-hot encoding spent up to four 24-bit masks. With a uniform 14-byte
stride, no level cap, and no per-group base pointers, the block shrinks to
4.804~b/weight and, because the kernel was memory-bound, runs
\emph{faster}: $1.14\times$ over \textsc{Slot32} at bit-identical decoded
content ($1.14\times$ in five independent jobs, union of ranges 1.13--1.15)
and $2.15\times$ over FP16. Achieved bandwidth is
unchanged (431 to 428~GB/s): the time falls exactly as the bytes do. That
invariance is also evidence about \emph{why}: two very different access
geometries achieve the same bandwidth, so record geometry is not what binds
in this range (\S\ref{sec:attribution}).

\textbf{\textsc{Planes12x}} pushes further with a sparse overlay: blocks
that fit a stricter level budget take a 12-byte fast path, and the rare
violators carry an exact side-channel correction. Reconstruction is
bit-exact, and this matters because the obvious alternative is not free:
\emph{hard-capping} the level count at four (which would allow a plain
two-plane 12-byte format at about 4.1~b/weight) was measured to cost
$+4.75\%$ perplexity, enough to fall behind QTIP. The overlay gets 4.342~b/weight at
$2.00\times$ with zero quality change.

\textbf{\textsc{Golay70}: a negative result, attacked twice.} The sign mask
is redundant: signs on the codeword support are determined up to the Golay
coset, so storing 12 code bits instead of 24 sign bits yields
3.589~b/weight, reconstruction-exact on all 150{,}681{,}600 blocks of the
artifact. But recovering the signs requires resolving the coset \emph{per
slot}, and the kernel stops being memory-bound: 199~GB/s, 30\% of its byte
bound, $1.34\times$, below the $1.6\times$ criterion fixed before
measuring, in the message of the commit that lands the
layout\ifanon{ (\texttt{caef2ac})}{}: an antecedence resting on a commit
date, where the replacement criterion below carries a trusted timestamp.

That failure had an identified cause, so we attacked it. Everything
distinguishing the two cosets is a function of the block's three 24-bit
words, so it hoists out of the slot loop into a per-block prologue of about
a dozen integer operations, leaving a per-slot path of three mask tests and
a negation; \emph{not one stored byte changes}, and the identity is proved
slot by slot and block by block. The repair works and is not enough: 199 to
264~GB/s, 30\% to 40\%, $1.34\times$ to $1.78\times$. Halving the per-slot
decode returned 2.01~ms of the 5.72~ms by which the layout missed its DRAM
floor: the diagnosis was right, the bar still missed. Where the rest sits is
visible too --- each arm stands above its own byte floor by 1.81, 2.51 and
3.72~ms for \textsc{Planes14}, \textsc{Planes12x} and the hoisted
\textsc{Golay70}, the excess over \textsc{Planes14}'s gap growing from
0.71~ms at a 3.4\% exception rate to 1.91 at 7.4\%, faster than in
proportion to a correction pass that lies outside the path this repair
touched. The design space is not empty; the layout missed a pre-registered
bar twice.

The bar had moved between the attempts, for a reason: once a deployed
4-bit kernel proved faster and smaller than our production layout (below), a
speed threshold could no longer settle what had become a memory argument.
The replacement ($\geq 2.0\times$, the speed of \textsc{Planes12x},
plus a $\geq 20\%$ whole-model memory margin) was timestamped before
measuring. The memory condition held by arithmetic; the speed condition
missed, $1.77\times$ against $2.0\times$ in the campaign that decided it
(Table~\ref{tab:layouts} re-measures the same kernel at $1.78\times$ ten
days later, which is the dispersion this section documents and not a second
verdict). Our estimate of
$1.9$--$2.4\times$, from an instruction count, was optimistic for the
reason flagged at the time as its weakest: integer and float issue share
ports.

The scale is brutally nonlinear: \emph{on a
memory-bound kernel, bits saved are speed gained, until the decode that
saves them stops being shifts and masks.} For lattice codes on this
hardware, that stops between 4.8 and 4.3~b/weight.

\textbf{Where a deployed 4-bit kernel sits on the same scale.} The last two
rows of Table~\ref{tab:layouts} are outside arms. The first is the AWQ
w4g128 GEMV kernel~\citep{awq2024}, ported into our harness unchanged (its
own grid, its own binary16 output) and timed in the same rounds and the same
process as ours. It reads 4.179~b/weight, less than three of our four byte
formats, so a ratio against FP16 flatters it by construction and we do not
lean on the $3.38\times$. Against the two \textsc{Golay70} rows, which
read less than it does, the same effect runs the other way and they lose
regardless. The comparable quantity is the fraction of the byte
bound, and it is the result of this section: \textbf{88\% for the 4-bit
kernel against 65\% for our best layout}. Both read a compressed format from
DRAM on the same card; one converts nearly all of its byte advantage into
time, the other two thirds. The gap is not the format but what happens
between the load and the multiply.

Two asymmetries qualify that number. The AWQ payload is \emph{synthesized}
(our own weights requantized to w4g128), which is sound for timing (the
kernel has no data-dependent branch, so its traffic depends only on the
shapes) and unsound for quality statements, which this arm makes none of.
And w4g128 quantizes every column, so it carries no tail where our rates do;
billing \textsc{Planes14} without its tail and row scales gives 63\% of
bound rather than 65\%, widening the gap to 25 points. We keep 65\%
because it is what the shipped format streams.

\subsection{And where the 2-bit state of the art sits}
\label{sec:qtip}

This paper's motivation is a sentence we inherited: the original LLVQ kernel
``is reported slower than QTIP's''. That is a citation, not a measurement.
The last row of Table~\ref{tab:layouts} closes it. We fetch the published
QTIP inference kernel at a pinned commit (GPL~v3, hash-verified, measured
but not redistributed), give it its own launch geometry exactly as shipped,
tune nothing in either direction, and time it in the same rounds and process
as everything else. Its payload is pseudo-random, sound for timing (no
data-dependent branch, so traffic depends only on the shapes) and unsound
for quality, on which this arm says nothing. All $1\,105\,920$ output rows
are checked against the f64 reference at \emph{our} tolerance, not the
looser one we concede to arms emitting binary16: worst error
$5.4\times10^{-8}$ against $10^{-5}$.

\textbf{The inherited claim holds, against us.} Formed as every other
comparison here --- same phase, same process, same shapes --- the QTIP
kernel runs $\mathbf{2.27\times}$ [2.27--2.28] faster than our production
layout.$^{\dagger}$ The three possible verdicts for this quantity were
written and timestamped before measuring; this is the one that says our
motivation was right about the world and wrong about us.

\textbf{The mechanism is neither quality nor the format's rate.} Our
artifact stores 2.07 bits per weight on disk (\S\ref{sec:evaluation}) and
QTIP's 2.000: as \emph{codes} these are the same operating point, and at 4B
they sit within noise of each other on perplexity (Appendix~\ref{app:scale}).
The gap opens at load time. A $\Lambda_{24}$ index is a combinatorial
address --- class, Golay codeword, mixed-radix rank, sign rules --- and this
section has now shown twice that it does not decode at memory speed
(\textsc{Golay70}, $1.34\times$ then $1.78\times$ against a $2.0\times$
bar). So the served path \emph{unfolds} it at load time into bit planes a
kernel reads with shifts and masks, and pays 4.804 b/weight in VRAM for
that. QTIP never unfolds: its state is a sliding 16-bit window over the
bitstream itself, consecutive states overlap by 12 bits, only $kV = 4$ new
bits are ever stored, and a weight decodes in a few instructions and one
lookup in a 2~KiB table. It reads 0.91~GB where we read 2.18 on the same
matrices. \emph{The Leech lattice packs as well as the trellis and unfolds
worse} --- not a quality deficit, not an implementation detail.

\textbf{It also breaks our own floor, which is the more useful result.} The
first row of Table~\ref{tab:layouts} is a kernel of ours running the same
252 launches over the same shapes and reading \emph{no weight bytes at
all}: 2.306~ms, $4.77\times$ FP16. We had treated that as a ceiling on what
any format could buy. The 2-bit competitor does the same projections,
reading 0.91~GB, in 2.246~ms --- faster than our kernel that reads nothing,
by 2.7\% against a resolution of 0.36\%. The floor was never a property of
the machine but of our launch geometry, one warp per output row across 252
launches, and a differently shaped kernel walks through it. That reframes
\S\ref{sec:attribution}: its largest term, unmasked latency and occupancy
at 39\% of the gap, is not a cost of lattice decoding, and the fact that it
has never been attacked becomes a measured opportunity rather than an
estimated one.

\textbf{And it marks the edge of this section's own metric.} The fraction of
the byte bound compares an arm to $16/b$ times the control, which is
interpretable only while $16/b$ stays under the floor, i.e.\ while
$b > 16/4.77 = 3.35$~b/weight. Every arm here had been above it; at 2.000,
QTIP is the first below, with a byte bound of $8.00\times$ against a floor
of $4.77\times$. We pre-registered the prediction that its fraction could
therefore not exceed 59.6\%, structurally, whatever the kernel; it measures
61\%. Both halves are worth keeping --- the metric does saturate as
predicted, and the prediction was still wrong, because it rested on a floor
we did not yet know was ours rather than the card's. Reading the last two
rows down the \emph{of bound} column would rank the 4-bit kernel best
(88\%), ours next (65\%) and the fastest kernel in the table worst (61\%):
true, and useless. Where the byte bounds are comparable that column is the
right instrument; at 2 bits the honest quantity is the time.

\subsection{How close to the floor? A measured attribution}
\label{sec:attribution}

``Bandwidth-dominated'' is a claim about a kernel, and we measured it. The instrumented job carries its own FP16 control
(7.27~GB at 662~GB/s, a rounding step from Table~\ref{tab:layouts}'s 661;
a floor from one process and a time from another is exactly the
subtraction this section's methodology forbids). Against it, the 2.50~GB
that \textsc{Slot32} reads put its DRAM floor at 3.78~ms, and the
instrumented arm takes 5.82~ms (5.820 in Table~\ref{tab:layouts}:
instrumentation costs nothing measurable): a gap of \textbf{2.04~ms} that belongs to the kernel alone. Three ``floor'' kernels, each adding one stage,
attribute it:

A source-level instruction count over the same kernel disagrees on every
line: it put bank conflicts at 0.3--2.9~ms (they are 0.118), overestimated
the table gather twelvefold, and declared arithmetic ``out of the picture''
when decode and the latency it fails to hide are the largest recoverable
terms.

One term is already recovered. Fusing $q/k/v$ and
$\textit{gate}/\textit{up}$ into row-concatenated launches takes the model
from 252 kernels to 144 and returns \textbf{0.803~ms}, 39\% of the gap,
output bit-identical on $921\,600$ rows and bytes read identical: a purely
geometric gain. We publish no cross-arm ratio from it --- the FP16 arm has
no fused counterpart, and the fused kernel is \textsc{Slot32}-only, off the
served path of \S\ref{sec:integration}.

The $2.15\times$ of Table~\ref{tab:layouts} is therefore not a ceiling
imposed by the format. The attribution is measured on \textsc{Slot32}, but
its largest term is launch geometry, which is orthogonal to the record
format, and roughly a third of the gap has never been attacked. The 4-bit
competitor's 88\% shows the same from outside: much of the 23 points between
us is not a property of lattice decoding at all. The 2-bit competitor shows
it from further outside still, and settles it: running the same 252
projections in its own grid, it finishes ahead of our kernel that reads no
weights (\S\ref{sec:qtip}), so the 39\% attributed here to unmasked latency
is recoverable by construction and not by a format.

\subsection{The same layouts on a second memory hierarchy}
\label{sec:a100}

Everything above is one card. Since a byte saved only becomes time saved if
the kernel is bandwidth-bound, the scale of \S\ref{sec:layouts} is a claim
about a \emph{pair}: format and memory hierarchy together. We tested that by re-running nine of these arms, from the same
binary and the same artifact, on an NVIDIA A100 (HBM2e, \texttt{sm\_80}) against the L40S of
Table~\ref{tab:layouts} (GDDR6, \texttt{sm\_89}). The kernels are compiled
for the target at startup; nothing in their text changed, and the
line-by-line f64 verification returns the \emph{same} worst errors on both
cards, so the arithmetic is identical and only the machine differs.

\textbf{The advantage does not transfer, and the GB/s column shows why.}
On the A100 no decoding arm reaches the FP16 baseline: our production
layout runs at $0.79\times$ where it ran at $2.15\times$. The mechanism is
not a faster baseline outrunning them --- the decoders got slower
\emph{in absolute terms} on the card with more bandwidth.
\textsc{Planes14} falls from 427 to 250~GB/s and every LLVQ arm falls with
it, while FP16 rises from 661 to 1052 and cuBLAS to 1204. A memory-bound
kernel cannot behave that way: these arms are compute-bound on the A100,
which raises the memory ceiling without raising per-SM decode throughput,
and they cross the ridge point of the roofline~\citep{roofline2009}. The
floor shows the same crossing from the other end: a kernel that reads no
weights is worth $4.77\times$ FP16 on the L40S and $1.68\times$ on the
A100, so what our launch geometry could buy shrinks from nearly five to under
two before a format is chosen. The 4-bit competitor crosses in the
same direction from much further away ($3.38\times$ to $1.82\times$),
which places the effect in the whole class of decoding kernels, lattice or
otherwise.

What survives is the \emph{ordering}: \textsc{Planes14} still leads, the
two \textsc{Golay70} decoders still trail, and v2 still beats v1, so the
format work of this section is not an artifact of one machine; and the
f64 check holds to the last digit. What is bounded is the headline:
\emph{decoding at matvec speed is a result about a bandwidth-bound regime},
and this second point measures where that regime ends. On an HBM-class card, expect the ordering of Table~\ref{tab:layouts}
and none of its ratios.
