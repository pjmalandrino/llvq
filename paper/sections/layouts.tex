\section{VRAM Layouts: Decoupling the Disk Rate from the Memory Rate}
\label{sec:layouts}

The rate that determines which models fit on a GPU is not the artifact's
on-disk rate but the rate of the format the kernel actually reads. This
section is an experimentally mapped scale of that trade-off: five layouts,
one benchmark harness, one byte-accounting convention.

\textbf{Methodology.} All five arms run in a single process on an NVIDIA
L40S, interleaved within every round in a fixed dispatch order; 7 rounds,
the first 2 discarded. Each reported speedup is the \emph{median of the
per-round ratios} with its range over the kept rounds, never a quotient
of two minima, which would compare rounds that did not coexist. Byte
accounting includes payload, per-class tables, the full-precision tail
columns, and per-row scales. All kernels use zero bytes of local memory.
Every row of every arm is verified against f64 before timing
(\S\ref{sec:decoder}). Data: \texttt{docs/data/echelle-formats.csv};
raw logs in \texttt{docs/mesures/}.

\textbf{\textsc{Slot32}} stores the decoded fields at fixed offsets inside a
bit-packed, byte-aligned record whose width is $9 + g + 24L$ bits: a 9-bit
class identifier, $g=1$ gain bit, a 24-bit sign mask, and one 24-bit one-hot
mask per non-zero magnitude level naming which coordinates carry it. That is
130 bits at the widest class ($L=5$) and 106 at $L=4$. The kernel addresses a
record by its byte offset and reads a fixed five-word (20~B) window, an
alignment bound the format guarantees rather than checks. It is the simplest
divergence-free format and our first to beat FP16 ($1.87\times$), but at
5.510~b/weight, records plus per-class tables, full-precision tail columns
and per-row scales, it is \emph{more} memory than a 4-bit format: fast and
pointless at scale.

\textbf{\textsc{Planes14}} is the observation that the four one-hot level
masks $m_1..m_4$ waste bits: blocks carry between three and five magnitude
levels, so three binary bit planes name each coordinate's level where the
one-hot encoding spent up to four 24-bit masks. With a uniform 14-byte
stride, no level cap, and no per-group base pointers, the block shrinks to
4.804~b/weight and, because the kernel was memory-bound, runs
\emph{faster}: $1.14\times$ over \textsc{Slot32} (ranges 1.13--1.15 across two
independent runs) at bit-identical decoded content, $2.14\times$ over FP16. Achieved bandwidth is
unchanged (428 to 425~GB/s): the time falls exactly as the bytes do. That
invariance is also evidence about \emph{why} the kernel sits where it does:
a uniform 14-byte stride and a bit-packed five-word window are very different
access geometries, and they achieve the same bandwidth, so record geometry is
not what binds in this range (\S\ref{sec:attribution}).

\textbf{\textsc{Planes12x}} pushes further with a sparse overlay: blocks
that fit a stricter level budget take a 12-byte fast path, and the rare
violators carry an exact side-channel correction. Reconstruction is
bit-exact, and this matters because the obvious alternative is not free:
\emph{hard-capping} the level count at four (which would allow a plain
two-plane 12-byte format at about 4.1~b/weight) was measured to cost
$+4.75\%$ perplexity, enough to fall behind QTIP. The overlay gets 4.342~b/weight at
$1.98\times$ with zero quality change.

\textbf{\textsc{Golay70}: a measured negative result.} The sign mask is
redundant: signs on the codeword support are determined up to the Golay
coset, so storing 12 code bits instead of 24 sign bits yields
3.589~b/weight, real and reconstruction-exact. But recovering the signs
requires a double-coset decode per block, and the kernel stops being
memory-bound: achieved bandwidth drops to 195~GB/s and the speedup to
$1.31\times$, below our $1.6\times$ acceptance criterion, fixed before the
measurement. We report it because it locates the floor of this design space:
past the knee, further rate reduction is no longer free; the decode
work itself becomes the bottleneck.

The scale is brutally nonlinear: \emph{on a
memory-bound kernel, bits saved are speed gained, until the decode that
saves them stops being shifts and masks.} For lattice codes on this
hardware, that stops between 4.8 and 4.3~b/weight.

\subsection{How close to the floor? A measured attribution}
\label{sec:attribution}

``Bandwidth-dominated'' is a claim about a kernel, and we measured it rather
than asserting it. The FP16 control reads 7.27~GB at 660~GB/s on this shape,
so the 2.50~GB that \textsc{Slot32} reads put its own DRAM floor at
$2.50/0.660 = 3.78$~ms. A separately instrumented run of the same two arms
takes 5.82~ms on the \textsc{Slot32} side (Table~\ref{tab:layouts} reports
5.883~ms and 428~GB/s for the uninstrumented one): a gap of
\textbf{2.04~ms} that belongs to the kernel, not to the memory system. Three
``floor'' kernels, each adding one stage, with per-shape CUDA events and
submission timing, attribute it:

This overturns a static audit we had written the same day from source-level
instruction counts: it put bank conflicts at 0.3--2.9~ms (they are 0.118),
overestimated the table gather twelvefold, and declared arithmetic ``out of
the picture'' when decode and the latency it fails to hide are in fact the
largest recoverable terms. \emph{A source-level count without SASS was wrong
by a factor of two on the one item it was most confident about}, which is why
the table above comes from an instrumented job and not from reasoning.

One term is already recovered. Fusing $q/k/v$ and
$\textit{gate}/\textit{up}$ into row-concatenated launches takes the model
from 252 kernels to 144 and returns \textbf{0.803~ms}, 39\% of the gap, with
output bit-identical on $921\,600$ rows and bytes read identical to 0.00\%:
a purely geometric gain. We deliberately do not publish the resulting
cross-arm ratio, because the FP16 arm has no fused counterpart (building one
costs 7.27~GB of additional VRAM), so the comparison would credit our arm
with an optimization the baseline was denied. The fused kernel is
\textsc{Slot32}-only and is not on the served path of
\S\ref{sec:integration}; as separate launches, $k/v$ are worth nothing
anyway (128 resident blocks out of an 852-block capacity, 157~GB/s against
469, $1.06\times$).

The consequence for the rest of this paper is that the $2.14\times$ of
Table~\ref{tab:layouts} is not a ceiling imposed by the format. The
attribution above is measured on \textsc{Slot32}, but its largest term is
launch geometry, which is orthogonal to the record format: rate reduction and
launch geometry are independent levers, and roughly a third of the remaining
gap has never been attacked.
