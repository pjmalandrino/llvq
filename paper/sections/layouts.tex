\section{VRAM Layouts: Decoupling the Disk Rate from the Memory Rate}
\label{sec:layouts}

The rate that determines which models fit on a GPU is not the artifact's
on-disk rate but the rate of the format the kernel actually reads. This
section is an experimentally mapped scale of that trade-off: four byte
formats of ours read by five kernels (one format gets two decoders), a
deployed 4-bit competitor, an FP16 control, one benchmark harness, one
byte-accounting convention.

\textbf{Methodology.} All arms run in a single process on an NVIDIA L40S,
interleaved within every round in a fixed dispatch order; 7 rounds, the first
2 discarded. Each reported speedup is the \emph{median of the per-round
ratios} with its range, never a quotient of two minima, which would compare
rounds that did not coexist. All kernels use zero bytes of local memory, and
every row of every arm is verified against f64 before timing
(\S\ref{sec:decoder}).

Byte accounting is the kernel's: what each arm streams per matrix --- payload,
exception side-channels, full-precision tail columns, per-row scales. Three
asymmetries follow, uncorrected. Per-class tables are excluded, being
resident constants of a few kilobytes against gigabytes of stream, though
\textsc{Golay70} alone loads two of them. The FP16 control bills its tail at
f16 where our arms bill theirs at f32, and the 4-bit competitor bills no tail
at all: both understate our arms. Bandwidth is the bench's
convention, computed from each arm's fastest kept round rather than its
median; on medians every reported percentage is unchanged.

Adding an arm perturbs the others, so the run has two phases: six arms,
then the same six plus the seventh. Across phases the six common ratios move
by at most 0.24\%, inside their worst intra-run half-range of 0.30\%, so
the addition is not resolvable and the incumbents' numbers are the same
object before and after. Data:
\texttt{docs/data/echelle-formats.csv}; raw log
\texttt{docs/mesures/golay70-v2-sept-bras-2026-08-11.txt}.

\textbf{\textsc{Slot32}} stores the decoded fields at fixed offsets inside a
bit-packed, byte-aligned record whose width is $9 + g + 24L$ bits: a 9-bit
class identifier, $g=1$ gain bit, a 24-bit sign mask, and one 24-bit one-hot
mask per non-zero magnitude level naming which coordinates carry it. That is
130 bits at the widest class ($L=5$) and 106 at $L=4$. The kernel addresses a
record by its byte offset and reads a fixed five-word (20~B) window, an
alignment bound the format guarantees rather than checks. It is the simplest
divergence-free format and our first to beat FP16 ($1.89\times$), but at
5.510~b/weight --- per the byte accounting above --- it is \emph{more}
memory than a 4-bit format: fast and pointless at scale.

\textbf{\textsc{Planes14}} is the observation that the four one-hot level
masks $m_1..m_4$ waste bits: blocks carry between three and five magnitude
levels, so three binary bit planes name each coordinate's level where the
one-hot encoding spent up to four 24-bit masks. With a uniform 14-byte
stride, no level cap, and no per-group base pointers, the block shrinks to
4.804~b/weight and, because the kernel was memory-bound, runs
\emph{faster}: $1.14\times$ over \textsc{Slot32} at bit-identical decoded
content ($1.14\times$ in five independent jobs, union of ranges 1.13--1.15)
and $2.15\times$ over FP16. Achieved bandwidth is
unchanged (429 to 427~GB/s): the time falls exactly as the bytes do. That
invariance is also evidence about \emph{why}: two very different access
geometries achieve the same bandwidth, so record geometry is not what binds
in this range (\S\ref{sec:attribution}).

\textbf{\textsc{Planes12x}} pushes further with a sparse overlay: blocks
that fit a stricter level budget take a 12-byte fast path, and the rare
violators carry an exact side-channel correction. Reconstruction is
bit-exact, and this matters because the obvious alternative is not free:
\emph{hard-capping} the level count at four (which would allow a plain
two-plane 12-byte format at about 4.1~b/weight) was measured to cost
$+4.75\%$ perplexity, enough to fall behind QTIP. The overlay gets 4.342~b/weight at
$2.00\times$ with zero quality change.

\textbf{\textsc{Golay70}: a negative result, attacked twice.} The sign mask
is redundant: signs on the codeword support are determined up to the Golay
coset, so storing 12 code bits instead of 24 sign bits yields
3.589~b/weight, reconstruction-exact on all 150{,}681{,}600 blocks of the
artifact. But recovering the signs requires resolving the coset \emph{per
slot}, and the kernel stops being memory-bound: 199~GB/s, 30\% of its byte
bound, $1.34\times$, below the $1.6\times$ criterion fixed before
measuring --- in the message of the commit that lands the layout
(\texttt{caef2ac}), so its antecedence rests on a commit date, not on the
trusted timestamp the replacement criterion below carries.

That failure had an identified cause, so we attacked it. Everything
distinguishing the two
cosets is a function of the block's three 24-bit words, so it hoists out of
the slot loop into a per-block prologue of about a dozen integer operations,
leaving a per-slot path of three mask tests and a negation --- and \emph{not
one stored byte changes}. The identity is proved slot by slot against the
original decode and block by block against the artifact. The repair works and
is not enough: 199 to 263~GB/s, 30\% to 40\%, $1.34\times$ to $1.77\times$.
Halving the per-slot decode returned 1.99~ms of the 5.74~ms by which the
layout missed its DRAM floor: the diagnosis was right, the bar still missed.

It also shows where the rest of the time is. Each arm sits above its own
floor (bytes over the control's 661~GB/s) by 1.82, 2.52 and 3.75~ms for
\textsc{Planes14}, \textsc{Planes12x} and the hoisted \textsc{Golay70};
the excess over \textsc{Planes14}'s gap is 0.70~ms at a 3.4\% exception
rate and 1.93 at 7.4\%, growing faster than in proportion to the correction
pass --- which lies outside the per-slot path this repair attacked and has
not been touched, nor has the per-block cost of the 9-byte stride. The
design space is not empty; the layout missed a pre-registered bar twice.

The bar had moved between the attempts, for a reason: once a deployed
4-bit kernel proved faster and smaller than our production layout (below), a
speed threshold could no longer settle what had become a memory argument.
The replacement --- $\geq 2.0\times$, the speed of \textsc{Planes12x},
plus a $\geq 20\%$ whole-model memory margin --- was timestamped before
measuring. The memory condition held by arithmetic; the speed condition
missed, $1.77\times$ against $2.0\times$. Our estimate of
$1.9$--$2.4\times$, from an instruction count, was optimistic for the
reason it had itself flagged as weakest: integer and float issue share
ports.

The scale is brutally nonlinear: \emph{on a
memory-bound kernel, bits saved are speed gained, until the decode that
saves them stops being shifts and masks.} For lattice codes on this
hardware, that stops between 4.8 and 4.3~b/weight.

\textbf{Where a deployed 4-bit kernel sits on the same scale.} The last row
of Table~\ref{tab:layouts} is not one of our layouts: it is the AWQ w4g128
GEMV kernel~\citep{awq2024}, ported into our harness unchanged --- its own
grid, its own binary16 output --- and timed in the same rounds and the same
process as ours. It reads 4.179~b/weight, less than three of our four byte
formats, so a ratio against FP16 flatters it by construction and we do not
lean on the $3.37\times$ --- against the two \textsc{Golay70} rows, which
read less than it does, the same effect runs the other way and they lose
regardless. The comparable quantity is the fraction of the byte
bound, and it is the result of this section: \textbf{88\% for the 4-bit
kernel against 65\% for our best layout}. Both read a compressed format from
DRAM on the same card; one converts nearly all of its byte advantage into
time, the other two thirds. The gap is not the format but what happens
between the load and the multiply.

Two asymmetries qualify that number. The AWQ payload is \emph{synthesized}
--- our own weights requantized to w4g128 --- which is sound for timing (the
kernel has no data-dependent branch, so its traffic depends only on the
shapes) and unsound for quality statements, which this arm makes none of.
And w4g128 quantizes every column, so it carries no tail where our rates do;
billing \textsc{Planes14} without its tail and row scales gives 63\% of
bound rather than 65\%, widening the gap to 25 points. We keep 65\%
because it is what the shipped format streams.

\subsection{How close to the floor? A measured attribution}
\label{sec:attribution}

``Bandwidth-dominated'' is a claim about a kernel, and we measured it
rather than asserting it. The instrumented job carries its own FP16 control
(7.27~GB at 662~GB/s, a rounding step from Table~\ref{tab:layouts}'s 661
--- a floor from one process and a time from another is exactly the
subtraction this section's methodology forbids). Against it, the 2.50~GB
that \textsc{Slot32} reads put its DRAM floor at 3.78~ms, and the
instrumented arm takes 5.82~ms (5.834 uninstrumented: instrumentation costs
nothing measurable): a gap of \textbf{2.04~ms} that belongs to the kernel,
not to the memory system. Three ``floor'' kernels, each adding one stage,
attribute it:

A source-level instruction count over the same kernel disagrees on every
line: it put bank conflicts at 0.3--2.9~ms (they are 0.118), overestimated
the table gather twelvefold, and declared arithmetic ``out of the picture''
when decode and the latency it fails to hide are the largest recoverable
terms. The table above comes from an instrumented job.

One term is already recovered. Fusing $q/k/v$ and
$\textit{gate}/\textit{up}$ into row-concatenated launches takes the model
from 252 kernels to 144 and returns \textbf{0.803~ms}, 39\% of the gap,
with output bit-identical on $921\,600$ rows and bytes read identical: a
purely geometric gain. We do not publish the resulting cross-arm ratio ---
the FP16 arm has no fused counterpart, so it would credit our arm with an
optimization the baseline was denied --- and the fused kernel is
\textsc{Slot32}-only, off the served path of \S\ref{sec:integration}.

The $2.15\times$ of Table~\ref{tab:layouts} is therefore not a ceiling
imposed by the format. The attribution is measured on \textsc{Slot32}, but
its largest term is launch geometry, which is orthogonal to the record
format, and roughly a third of the gap has never been attacked. The 4-bit
competitor's 88\% says the same from outside: much of the 23 points between
us is not a property of lattice decoding at all.

\subsection{The same layouts on a second memory hierarchy}
\label{sec:a100}

Everything above is one card. Since a byte saved only becomes time saved if
the kernel is bandwidth-bound, the scale of \S\ref{sec:layouts} is a claim
about a \emph{pair} --- format and memory hierarchy --- and not about the
format alone. We tested that by re-running the same nine arms, from the same
binary and the same artifact, on an NVIDIA A100 (HBM2e, \texttt{sm\_80}) against the L40S of
Table~\ref{tab:layouts} (GDDR6, \texttt{sm\_89}). The kernels are compiled
for the target at startup; nothing in their text changed, and the
line-by-line f64 verification returns the \emph{same} worst errors on both
cards, so the arithmetic is identical and only the machine differs.

\textbf{The advantage does not transfer, and the GB/s column says why.}
On the A100 no decoding arm reaches the FP16 baseline: our production
layout runs at $0.79\times$ where it ran at $2.15\times$. The mechanism is
not a faster baseline outrunning them --- the decoders got slower
\emph{in absolute terms} on the card with more bandwidth.
\textsc{Planes14} falls from 427 to 250~GB/s and every LLVQ arm falls with
it, while FP16 rises from 661 to 1052 and cuBLAS to 1204. A memory-bound
kernel cannot behave that way: these arms are compute-bound on the A100,
which raises the memory ceiling without raising per-SM decode throughput,
and they cross the ridge point of the roofline~\citep{roofline2009}. The
floor shows the same crossing from the other end --- a kernel that reads no
weights is worth $4.78\times$ FP16 on the L40S and $1.68\times$ on the
A100, so what \emph{any} format could buy shrinks from nearly five to under
two before a format is chosen --- and the 4-bit competitor crosses in the
same direction from much further away ($3.37\times$ to $1.82\times$),
which places the effect in the class of decoding kernels rather than in
lattice decoding.

What survives is the \emph{ordering}: \textsc{Planes14} still leads, the
two \textsc{Golay70} decoders still trail, and v2 still beats v1 --- so the
format work of this section is not an artifact of one machine --- and the
f64 check holds to the last digit. What is bounded is the headline:
\emph{decoding at matvec speed is a result about a bandwidth-bound regime},
and this second point measures where that regime ends rather than extending
it. On an HBM-class card, expect the ordering of Table~\ref{tab:layouts}
and none of its ratios.
