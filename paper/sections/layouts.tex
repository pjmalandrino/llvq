\section{VRAM Layouts: Decoupling the Disk Rate from the Memory Rate}
\label{sec:layouts}

The rate that determines which models fit on a GPU is not the artifact's
on-disk rate but the rate of the format the kernel actually reads. This
section is an experimentally mapped scale of that trade-off: five layouts of
ours, one deployed 4-bit competitor, one benchmark harness, one
byte-accounting convention.

\textbf{Methodology.} All arms run in a single process on an NVIDIA
L40S, interleaved within every round in a fixed dispatch order; 7 rounds,
the first 2 discarded. Each reported speedup is the \emph{median of the
per-round ratios} with its range over the kept rounds, never a quotient
of two minima, which would compare rounds that did not coexist. Byte
accounting is the kernel's: payload, per-class tables, the full-precision
tail columns, and per-row scales --- not the artifact's disk rate. All
kernels use zero bytes of local memory. Every row of every arm is verified
against f64 before timing (\S\ref{sec:decoder}).

Adding an arm perturbs the ones already there, so the run reported here is
two phases of one process: the six previously published arms, then the same
six \emph{plus} the seventh, uploaded between phases. The six common arms
move by at most 0.24\% in their ratio to the control across the two phases,
against a worst intra-run half-range of 0.56\%, so the addition is not
resolvable at this bench's resolution and the incumbents' numbers are the
same object before and after. We report that check because an earlier
six-arm job could not run it --- the harness had no way to leave an arm out,
so its control came from a different process --- and a rule broken twice
stops being a rule. Data: \texttt{docs/data/echelle-formats.csv}; raw log
\texttt{docs/mesures/golay70-v2-sept-bras-2026-08-11.txt}.

\textbf{\textsc{Slot32}} stores the decoded fields at fixed offsets inside a
bit-packed, byte-aligned record whose width is $9 + g + 24L$ bits: a 9-bit
class identifier, $g=1$ gain bit, a 24-bit sign mask, and one 24-bit one-hot
mask per non-zero magnitude level naming which coordinates carry it. That is
130 bits at the widest class ($L=5$) and 106 at $L=4$. The kernel addresses a
record by its byte offset and reads a fixed five-word (20~B) window, an
alignment bound the format guarantees rather than checks. It is the simplest
divergence-free format and our first to beat FP16 ($1.89\times$), but at
5.510~b/weight, records plus per-class tables, full-precision tail columns
and per-row scales, it is \emph{more} memory than a 4-bit format: fast and
pointless at scale.

\textbf{\textsc{Planes14}} is the observation that the four one-hot level
masks $m_1..m_4$ waste bits: blocks carry between three and five magnitude
levels, so three binary bit planes name each coordinate's level where the
one-hot encoding spent up to four 24-bit masks. With a uniform 14-byte
stride, no level cap, and no per-group base pointers, the block shrinks to
4.804~b/weight and, because the kernel was memory-bound, runs
\emph{faster}: $1.14\times$ over \textsc{Slot32} (ranges 1.13--1.15 across
three independent runs) at bit-identical decoded content, $2.15\times$ over
FP16. Achieved bandwidth is
unchanged (429 to 427~GB/s): the time falls exactly as the bytes do. That
invariance is also evidence about \emph{why} the kernel sits where it does:
a uniform 14-byte stride and a bit-packed five-word window are very different
access geometries, and they achieve the same bandwidth, so record geometry is
not what binds in this range (\S\ref{sec:attribution}).

\textbf{\textsc{Planes12x}} pushes further with a sparse overlay: blocks
that fit a stricter level budget take a 12-byte fast path, and the rare
violators carry an exact side-channel correction. Reconstruction is
bit-exact, and this matters because the obvious alternative is not free:
\emph{hard-capping} the level count at four (which would allow a plain
two-plane 12-byte format at about 4.1~b/weight) was measured to cost
$+4.75\%$ perplexity, enough to fall behind QTIP. The overlay gets 4.342~b/weight at
$2.00\times$ with zero quality change.

\textbf{\textsc{Golay70}: a negative result, attacked twice.} The sign mask
is redundant: signs on the codeword support are determined up to the Golay
coset, so storing 12 code bits instead of 24 sign bits yields
3.589~b/weight, real and reconstruction-exact on all 150{,}681{,}600 blocks
of the artifact. But recovering the signs requires resolving the coset
\emph{per slot}, and the kernel stops being memory-bound: 199~GB/s, 30\% of
its byte bound, $1.34\times$, below the $1.6\times$ acceptance criterion we
had fixed before measuring it.

That first result diagnosed a cause rather than merely recording a failure,
so we repaired it. Everything that distinguishes the two cosets is a
function of the block's three 24-bit words, so it hoists out of the slot
loop into a per-block prologue of about a dozen integer operations: the
per-slot path then becomes a \textsc{Planes14} slot with three masks instead
of four --- no variable shift, no coset select --- and \emph{not one stored
byte changes}. The identity is proved slot by slot against the original
decode and block by block against the artifact. The repair works, and it is
not enough: 199 to 263~GB/s, 30\% to 40\% of the bound, $1.34\times$ to
$1.77\times$. It confirms the diagnosis (halving the decode work moved the
achieved bandwidth by exactly the amount a decode-bound kernel should move)
while leaving the layout below the bar --- and it exhausts the obvious
repairs, since the per-slot path is now identical to that of a layout we
already ship.

We report both attempts, and we report that the second was judged by a
criterion committed before it ran. The original $1.6\times$ was a
\emph{speed} threshold; once a deployed 4-bit kernel proved faster and
smaller than our production layout (below), speed stopped being the question
this format was competing on, and a speed threshold could no longer settle a
memory argument. So we retired that criterion for cause, published a
replacement --- adoption requires $\geq 2.0\times$ (the speed of a layout we
already serve) \emph{and} a whole-model memory margin of $\geq 20\%$ over
the deployed 4-bit checkpoint --- and timestamped it before the measurement.
The memory condition was already satisfied by arithmetic; the speed
condition missed, at $1.77\times$ against $2.0\times$. Our own pre-measurement
estimate had been $1.9$--$2.4\times$, from an instruction count: it was
optimistic, for the reason the estimate itself flagged as its weakest
assumption, that integer and float issue share ports.

The scale is brutally nonlinear: \emph{on a
memory-bound kernel, bits saved are speed gained, until the decode that
saves them stops being shifts and masks.} For lattice codes on this
hardware, that stops between 4.8 and 4.3~b/weight.

\textbf{Where a deployed 4-bit kernel sits on the same scale.} The last row
of Table~\ref{tab:layouts} is not one of our layouts: it is the AWQ
w4g128 GEMV kernel~\citep{awq2024}, ported into our harness unchanged --- its
own grid, its own binary16 output --- and timed in the same rounds, the same
process and the same chronometer as ours. It reads 4.179~b/weight, less than
four of our five layouts, so a ratio against FP16 flatters it by
construction and we do not lean on the $3.37\times$. The number that
compares is the fraction of the byte bound, and it is the result of this
section: \textbf{88\% for the 4-bit kernel against 65\% for our best
layout}. Both kernels are reading a compressed format from DRAM on the same
card; one converts nearly all of its byte advantage into time and the other
converts two thirds. The gap is not the format, it is what happens between
the load and the multiply.

Two reserves, stated where the number is. The AWQ arm's payload is
\emph{synthesized} --- our own reconstructed weights, requantized to w4g128
--- and not the official checkpoint. That is legitimate for timing precisely
because the kernel has no data-dependent branch (fixed \texttt{\#pragma
unroll} bounds, no variable stride, no exception path), so its traffic and
its time depend only on the shapes; it is not legitimate for a quality
statement, and this arm carries none. And the byte columns are not
symmetric: w4g128 quantizes every column, so it has no tail policy and pays
no tail, where our arms carry full-precision tail columns in their rate.
That asymmetry runs against us, which is why it is disclosed rather than
corrected away.

\subsection{How close to the floor? A measured attribution}
\label{sec:attribution}

``Bandwidth-dominated'' is a claim about a kernel, and we measured it rather
than asserting it. The FP16 control reads 7.27~GB at 661~GB/s on this shape,
so the 2.50~GB that \textsc{Slot32} reads put its own DRAM floor at
$2.50/0.661 = 3.78$~ms. A separately instrumented run of the same two arms
takes 5.82~ms on the \textsc{Slot32} side (Table~\ref{tab:layouts} reports
5.834~ms and 429~GB/s for the uninstrumented one): a gap of
\textbf{2.04~ms} that belongs to the kernel, not to the memory system. Three
``floor'' kernels, each adding one stage, with per-shape CUDA events and
submission timing, attribute it:

This overturns a static audit we had written the same day from source-level
instruction counts: it put bank conflicts at 0.3--2.9~ms (they are 0.118),
overestimated the table gather twelvefold, and declared arithmetic ``out of
the picture'' when decode and the latency it fails to hide are in fact the
largest recoverable terms. \emph{A source-level count without SASS was wrong
by a factor of two on the one item it was most confident about}, which is why
the table above comes from an instrumented job and not from reasoning.

One term is already recovered. Fusing $q/k/v$ and
$\textit{gate}/\textit{up}$ into row-concatenated launches takes the model
from 252 kernels to 144 and returns \textbf{0.803~ms}, 39\% of the gap, with
output bit-identical on $921\,600$ rows and bytes read identical to 0.00\%:
a purely geometric gain. We deliberately do not publish the resulting
cross-arm ratio, because the FP16 arm has no fused counterpart (building one
costs 7.27~GB of additional VRAM), so the comparison would credit our arm
with an optimization the baseline was denied. The fused kernel is
\textsc{Slot32}-only and is not on the served path of
\S\ref{sec:integration}; as separate launches, $k/v$ are worth nothing
anyway (128 resident blocks out of an 852-block capacity, 157~GB/s against
469, $1.06\times$).

The consequence for the rest of this paper is that the $2.15\times$ of
Table~\ref{tab:layouts} is not a ceiling imposed by the format. The
attribution above is measured on \textsc{Slot32}, but its largest term is
launch geometry, which is orthogonal to the record format: rate reduction and
launch geometry are independent levers, and roughly a third of the remaining
gap has never been attacked. The 4-bit competitor's 88\% says the same thing
from outside, and says it more sharply than our own instrumentation did: the
23 points between its conversion rate and ours are not a property of
lattice decoding as such, since a large part of the gap we can name ---
launch geometry --- has nothing to do with decoding at all.
