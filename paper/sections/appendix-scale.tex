\section{The scale curve, and why its two metrics disagree}
\label{app:scale}

This appendix carries what \S\ref{sec:evaluation} summarizes: what the three
model sizes do to quality, whether the closing of the gap to 4 bits itself
slows, and why the answer depends on the metric.

\textbf{Does the closing itself slow? On the MMLU gap, the intervals
do not separate it.} Composing the two standard errors in quadrature
(independent campaigns; no cross-size pairing for a question set), the
gap falls by 6.96 points from 4B to 8B ($\mathrm{SE}$ 1.82, $p = 0.0001$),
by 1.40 from 8B to 14B ($\mathrm{SE}$ 1.68, $p = 0.40$), and by 8.36 end to
end ($p \approx 10^{-5}$): the first and the total closing are established,
the second step is not. \emph{And $p = 0.40$ is not evidence
of constancy either}: that step is uninformative on this metric, and both a
flattening curve and one closing at a smaller pace remain compatible with
it. These $z$-tests come from the intervals of
Table~\ref{tab:scale}; no further measurement lies behind them.

\begin{sloppypar}
\textbf{Perplexity gets error bars, at all three sizes.} Nine paired
intervals, three per model, cover the 12 evaluation windows (same text in
every arm, matching fingerprint): a Student $t$ interval on the mean
per-window NLL difference, 11 degrees of freedom, exponentiated to a
perplexity ratio (every window scores the same token count). As excess
over each reference: at 4B, AWQ/FP16 $+10.49\%$ $[+8.55, +12.47]$,
2-bit/FP16 $+38.45\%$ $[+33.62, +43.45]$, 2-bit/AWQ $+25.31\%$
$[+20.01, +30.84]$; at 8B, $+4.80\%$ $[+4.24, +5.35]$, $+22.01\%$
$[+19.37, +24.70]$ and $+16.42\%$
$[+14.17, +18.72]$; at 14B, $+3.81\%$ $[+3.27, +4.34]$, $+18.94\%$
$[+17.22, +20.68]$ and $+14.58\%$ $[+13.10, +16.08]$. None contains zero, and
all 108 window-level comparisons point the same way. The instrument returns a
null when it should: the same 8B file with an f16 and with an int8
embedding differs by $+0.0004\%$, $[-0.025, +0.026]$, containing zero.
\end{sloppypar}

\textbf{The 4B's three intervals rest on raw output, not a summary.}
The per-window lines are committed, not cited by job identifier
(\path{docs/mesures/a4-campagne-4b-ppl-BRUT-2026-08-06.txt}); three
controls ran first: the published perplexities replay from the NLLs to the
ten-thousandth; the paired differences are additive window by window (all
twelve residuals exactly zero in double precision, which a one-window offset
would break and nothing else would show); and the run fingerprint matches the
8B and 14B campaigns.

One reservation bounds all nine intervals: they cover the sampling of the
evaluation corpus and nothing else --- in particular not the calibration
draw, one seed per artifact and unmeasured at these scales --- so they
condition on the three files we produced. Both steps are
barred: the 8B-to-14B fall of the excess over FP16 is $-13.9\%$
$[-22.8, -4.9]$, the 4B-to-8B fall $-42.8\%$
$[-51.8, -33.5]$ --- the bar the previously circulated $-43\%$ figure
lacked. Against the AWQ reference (the product argument's), the
8B-to-14B step reads $-1.58\%$
$[-3.14, -0.004]$, $t = 2.2063$ against a threshold of 2.2010: it clears
zero by five thousandths, and must not be called a significant closing. \emph{Those
two percentages are in different parameterizations and must not be read as a
ninefold difference}: $-13.9\%$ is the fall of the \emph{excess}, $-1.58\%$ the
fall of the \emph{ratio} of perplexities, the only form the journal
publishes for the AWQ reference. In that parameterization the FP16
reference reads $-2.51\%$ $[-4.12, -0.88]$.
Data: \texttt{docs/\allowbreak mesures/\allowbreak
ppl-appariee-8b-14b-2026-08-17.txt} and
\texttt{docs/\allowbreak mesures/\allowbreak ppl-appariee-4b-2026-08-17.txt},
carried by \texttt{docs/data/ppl-appariee.csv}.

\textbf{On perplexity the closing does slow, and the knee resolves.} Here
the three campaigns \emph{can} be paired across model sizes (the 12 windows
are the same text at every size, common fingerprint), so the difference of
differences is formed window by window, no standard errors composed.
Against FP16 the perplexity \emph{ratio} falls by $\times0.8812$ $[0.8561, 0.9071]$ from 4B to 8B, all 12 windows
agreeing in sign, and $\times0.9749$ $[0.9588, 0.9912]$ from 8B to 14B, 10 of
12 agreeing. \emph{These are the two steps of Table~\ref{tab:scale} in the
other parameterization}: 0.8812
is the ratio of two perplexity ratios, $-42.8\%$ the fall of the excess.
The test runs on the log ratio, where per-window differences are linear,
making the pairing exact. The knee, the difference of
those two log ratios, is
$-0.1010$ $[-0.1377, -0.0643]$, $t = -6.06$ on 11 degrees of freedom, 11 of 12
windows agreeing: the first step lowers the excess strictly harder than the
second, the near edge nearly four standard errors from
zero. Against the AWQ reference the test still resolves, far less
comfortably ($-0.0576$ $[-0.1011, -0.0140]$, $t = -2.91$), and must not
be quoted with the confidence of the FP16 line. Data:
\texttt{docs/data/ppl-genou.csv}.

\textbf{Why the two metrics answer differently, and what neither buys.}
They are two quantities, not two measurements of one; the likeliest
explanation is power: perplexity averages 49\,140 scored tokens and
pairs its windows across sizes, MMLU rates 2\,280 questions with no
cross-size pairing. Two further differences are real: the columns measure
different faculties (2 bits damages reasoning far more than recall,
\S\ref{sec:limitations}) and compare different arms (2-bit
against FP16 versus against the 4-bit baseline --- checkable, and
insufficient on its own to carry the divergence, since the perplexity knee
still resolves against the AWQ reference). Every statement about the
knee in this paper therefore names its metric. Neither answer buys the
extrapolation: the perplexity knee conditions on three artifacts with
one calibration draw each, and a resolved knee establishes only that the
first step fell harder than the second, leaving open where the curve
flattens. We publish no scaling law from three models; a 32B point would
settle the shape.

\textbf{Against the published 2-bit methods.} Table~\ref{tab:lit} adds the
comparison at exactly matched rate: every 2-bit row spends 48 bits per 24-weight
block, the configurations differing only in the index/gain split ($48{+}0$,
$47{+}1$, $46{+}2$ in its Table~8); ours is the $47{+}1$ line its distortion
study ranks first, while its LLM results use the other two. What our sealed file
adds on top --- full-precision tail columns and per-row
scales, about 8\% on the 4B, less on the 8B whose \texttt{down\_proj} is
block-aligned --- sits outside the comparison: the original
paper never states its tail policy.

Absolute perplexities do not cross harnesses; the two baselines differ by
1.4\% on the 4B and agree on the 8B to the two decimals the original paper
prints, making the ratio column comparable. The verdict is the one
\S\ref{sec:evaluation} summarizes: level at 4B with the nearest
configurations, behind both LLVQ variants at 8B (0.78 perplexity points on a
shared baseline), ahead of QTIP alone there, and a wider MMLU shortfall
(55.59 against 60.7).

We claim no statistical significance for these differences: the original
paper publishes no error bars, our $\pm$ is single-arm sampling error, and
question samples differ across papers. The matched payload establishes
that the 8B shortfall is not bought back by a rate advantage.
We do not explain it; the natural suspect, roughly $100\times$ less
calibration data, is bounded in \S\ref{sec:limitations} at an effect too
small to be the whole story; the bound is on perplexity, which does not
constrain MMLU.
