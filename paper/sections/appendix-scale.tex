\section{The scale curve, and why its two metrics disagree}
\label{app:scale}

This appendix carries the characterization that \S\ref{sec:evaluation}
summarizes: what the three model sizes do to quality, whether the closing of
the gap to 4 bits itself slows, and why the answer depends on the metric. It
lives here because none of it is a statement about the kernel or the format;
it is the context in which the 2-bit operating point is or is not worth its
kernel.

\textbf{Does the closing itself slow? On the MMLU gap, the bars decline
to say.} Composing the two standard errors in quadrature (the campaigns are
independent; no pairing across model sizes exists for a question set), the
gap falls by 6.96 points from 4B to 8B ($\mathrm{SE}$ 1.82, $p = 0.0001$),
by 1.40 from 8B to 14B ($\mathrm{SE}$ 1.68, $p = 0.40$), and by 8.36 end to
end ($p \approx 10^{-5}$): the first closing and the total closing are
established, the second step is not. \emph{And $p = 0.40$ is not evidence
of constancy either} --- on that step this metric is silent, and a curve
that flattens and one that keeps closing at a smaller pace both remain
compatible with it. These are $z$-tests computed from the intervals of
Table~\ref{tab:scale}, not a further measurement; the same question put to
perplexity answers differently below.

\begin{sloppypar}
\textbf{Perplexity gets error bars, at all three sizes.} Nine paired
intervals exist, three per model, formed window by window over the 12
evaluation windows (the same text in every arm, guaranteed by the matching
fingerprint) as a Student $t$ interval on the mean per-window NLL
difference, 11 degrees of freedom, exponentiated exactly to a perplexity
ratio since every window scores the same token count. As excess over the
stated reference: at 4B, AWQ over FP16 $+10.49\%$ $[+8.55, +12.47]$, 2-bit
over FP16 $+38.45\%$ $[+33.62, +43.45]$, 2-bit over AWQ $+25.31\%$
$[+20.01, +30.84]$; at 8B, $+4.80\%$ $[+4.24, +5.35]$, $+22.01\%$
$[+19.37, +24.70]$ and $+16.42\%$
$[+14.17, +18.72]$; at 14B, $+3.81\%$ $[+3.27, +4.34]$, $+18.94\%$
$[+17.22, +20.68]$ and $+14.58\%$ $[+13.10, +16.08]$. None contains zero, and
all 108 window-level comparisons point the same way. The instrument returns a
null when it should: the same 8B file scored with an f16 and with an int8
embedding differs by $+0.0004\%$, interval $[-0.025, +0.026]$, which contains
zero.
\end{sloppypar}

\textbf{The 4B's three intervals rest on raw output, not on a summary.}
The per-window lines are committed rather than cited by job identifier
(\path{docs/mesures/a4-campagne-4b-ppl-BRUT-2026-08-06.txt}), and three
controls ran before they
were used: the published perplexities replay from the NLLs to the
ten-thousandth, the paired differences are additive window by window (all
twelve residuals exactly zero in double precision, which a one-window offset
would break and nothing else would show), and the run fingerprint matches
the 8B and 14B campaigns.

One reservation bounds all nine intervals: they cover the sampling of the
evaluation corpus and nothing else, in particular not the calibration draw,
which is one seed per artifact and is unmeasured at these scales; they
therefore condition on the three files we produced. Both steps of the curve
are barred. The
8B-to-14B fall of the excess over FP16 is $-13.9\%$ with interval
$[-22.8, -4.9]$, and the 4B-to-8B fall is $-42.8\%$ with interval
$[-51.8, -33.5]$ --- the bar that the $-43\%$ quoted in this repository since
2026-08-10 had gone without. Against the AWQ reference --- the one the product
argument rests on --- the 8B-to-14B step reads $-1.58\%$ with interval
$[-3.14, -0.004]$,
$t = 2.2063$ against a threshold of 2.2010, and it clears zero by five
thousandths, so it must not be described as a significant closing. \emph{Those
two percentages are in different parameterizations and must not be read as a
ninefold difference}: $-13.9\%$ is the fall of the \emph{excess}, $-1.58\%$ the
fall of the \emph{ratio} of perplexities, which is the only form the journal
publishes for the AWQ reference. In the ratio parameterization the two
references read $-2.51\%$ $[-4.12, -0.88]$ and $-1.58\%$ $[-3.14, -0.004]$.
Data: \texttt{docs/\allowbreak mesures/\allowbreak
ppl-appariee-8b-14b-2026-08-17.txt} and
\texttt{docs/\allowbreak mesures/\allowbreak ppl-appariee-4b-2026-08-17.txt},
carried by \texttt{docs/data/ppl-appariee.csv}.

\textbf{On perplexity the closing does slow, and the knee resolves.} The
question put to the MMLU gap above can now be put to this
column, and here the three campaigns \emph{can} be paired across model sizes:
the 12 windows are the same text at every size, which the common fingerprint
guarantees, so the difference of differences is formed window by window rather
than by composing two standard errors. Against FP16 the perplexity
\emph{ratio} falls by a
factor $\times0.8812$ $[0.8561, 0.9071]$ from 4B to 8B, all 12 windows
agreeing in sign, and $\times0.9749$ $[0.9588, 0.9912]$ from 8B to 14B, 10 of
12 agreeing. \emph{Those are the same two steps as the $-42.8\%$ and
$-13.9\%$ of Table~\ref{tab:scale}, in the other parameterization}: 0.8812 is
the ratio of two perplexity ratios, $-42.8\%$ the fall of the excess, and the
warning two paragraphs above applies here too --- the test is run on the log
ratio because that is what the per-window differences are linear in, which is
what makes the pairing exact. The knee is the difference of those two log
ratios, and it is
$-0.1010$ $[-0.1377, -0.0643]$, $t = -6.06$ on 11 degrees of freedom, 11 of 12
windows agreeing: the first step lowers the excess strictly harder than the
second, with the near edge of the interval nearly four standard errors from
zero. Against the AWQ reference the same test still resolves but far less
comfortably --- $-0.0576$ $[-0.1011, -0.0140]$, $t = -2.91$ --- and it must not
be quoted with the confidence of the FP16 line. Data:
\texttt{docs/data/ppl-genou.csv}.

\textbf{Why the two metrics answer differently, and what neither buys.}
They are two quantities, not two measurements of one. The likeliest
explanation is power --- perplexity averages 49\,140 scored tokens and
pairs its windows across sizes, MMLU rates 2\,280 questions with no
cross-size pairing --- but two further differences are real: the columns do
not measure the same faculty (2 bits damages reasoning far more than recall,
\S\ref{sec:limitations}), and they do not compare the same arms (2-bit
against FP16 versus against the 4-bit baseline; checkable, and not carrying
the divergence alone, since against the AWQ reference the perplexity knee
still resolves). \emph{Hence every statement about the knee in this paper
names its metric.} What neither answer buys is the extrapolation: the
perplexity knee conditions on three artifacts with one calibration draw
each, and a resolved knee says the first step fell harder than the second,
not where the curve flattens. We publish no scaling law from three models; a
32B point is what would settle the shape.

\textbf{Against the published 2-bit methods.} Table~\ref{tab:lit} adds
the comparison a reader of the original paper will make anyway, at exactly
matched rate: every 2-bit row spends 48 bits per 24-weight block, the
configurations differing only in the index/gain split ($48{+}0$, $47{+}1$,
$46{+}2$ in its Table~8); ours is the $47{+}1$ line its own distortion study
ranks first, while its LLM results use the other two. What our sealed file
adds on top of that payload --- full-precision tail columns and per-row
scales, about 8\% on the 4B, less on the 8B whose \texttt{down\_proj} is
exactly block-aligned --- sits outside the comparison, since the original
paper never states its tail policy. The code rate can be compared, and it is
equal.

Absolute perplexities do not cross harnesses, so each $\times$ is the
ratio to its own harness's FP16 baseline --- and the two baselines differ by
1.4\% on the 4B and agree on the 8B to the two decimals the original paper
prints, which is what makes the column comparable. The verdict is the one
\S\ref{sec:evaluation} summarizes: level at 4B with the nearest
configurations, behind both LLVQ variants at 8B (0.78 perplexity points on a
shared baseline), ahead of QTIP alone there, and a wider MMLU shortfall
(55.59 against 60.7).

We claim no statistical significance for these differences: the original
paper publishes no error bars, our $\pm$ is single-arm sampling error, and
across papers the question samples differ. What the matched payload does
establish is that the 8B shortfall is not bought back by a rate advantage.
We do not explain it; the natural suspect, roughly $100\times$ less
calibration data, is bounded in \S\ref{sec:limitations} at an effect too
small to be the whole story --- on perplexity, which does not constrain
MMLU.
