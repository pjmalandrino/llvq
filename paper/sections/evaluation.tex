\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

\textbf{Protocol.} All quality numbers come from one harness on one machine
(NVIDIA L40S), f16 on both sides of every comparison, with token-level
fingerprints printed on each result line and required to match across arms:
perplexity fingerprint \texttt{3f1baca9033bf251} (WikiText-2
test~\citep{wikitext2016}, context 4096, 12 windows), MMLU
fingerprint \texttt{65dcd53655e8bfa5} (2\,280 questions sampled 40 per
subject from a fixed seed~\citep{mmlu2021}, micro-averaged, the original
paper's convention; macro-averaging inflates the quantized arm's deficit
and is not reported here). The harness is validated by its FP16 arm: MMLU
$70.32 \pm 1.28$ on Qwen3-4B~\citep{qwen3_2025} against the 70.2 that the
original paper's Table~6 reports for the same
model~\citep{llvq2026}. The quantized weights are the sealed artifact
(47-bit indices $+$ 1 gain bit, produced by Spherical GPTQ with input
rotation and 131k tokens of C4 calibration), and the evaluation scores
files, not in-memory reconstructions.

\textbf{What the serving stack does and does not change.} Columns 3 and 4
serve the same quantized linears. The layout transcoding is proved a
bijection of the decoded content on all 150{,}681{,}600 blocks of the
artifact, every row of every arm is verified against an f64 reference
before any timing (\S\ref{sec:decoder}), and the fused and dense engines
emit identical greedy tokens up to a tie-break-level divergence. The
quality scores themselves run through the dense evaluation path; what the
table shows directly is the cost of the int8 embedding, the one lossy
change on the serving path: 16.9358 versus 16.9422 perplexity and $55.70$
versus $55.59$ MMLU, both within sampling noise. The $2.03\times$
throughput and $3.09\times$ VRAM reduction ride on content proved
bit-identical plus an embedding change measured to be free.

\textbf{Memory accounting: two denominators, always labelled.} Model-level
comparisons --- this section, the abstract's serving figures, the limitations
--- are in bits per parameter over the \emph{whole model, embedding
included}. Per-weight rates over the quantized linears alone overstate
compression badly on small models (the 4B's embedding is 9.7\% of its
parameters) and silently mix denominators against other methods' figures. On
this accounting the served footprint is 5.15~b/param, below the official AWQ
4-bit checkpoint's 5.30 in its own engine. \S\ref{sec:layouts} necessarily
uses the other denominator --- what the kernel reads per quantized projection
weight, the quantity a bandwidth argument is about --- and there the same
competitor reads \emph{less} than we do (4.18 against 4.80). Both are true of
different questions: what fits on the card, and what the matvec streams. They
are never subtracted from one another.

\textbf{What 2 bits costs.} Against FP16 the price is $\times1.384$
perplexity and $-14.6$ points of MMLU. The AWQ arm makes the comparison
sharp: at 4 bits on this model, quality is essentially free
($\times1.105$, $-0.3$~points). On a 4B, 4-bit dominates 2-bit on every
axis except disk; \S\ref{sec:limitations} discusses where the 2-bit point
can still win.

\textbf{The scale point.} Table~\ref{tab:campaign8b} is the paper's second
model, requantized under the exact 4B configuration. Three movements
matter, and they all point the same way. The 2-bit degradation falls with
scale, on both axes: $\times1.384 \to \times1.220$ perplexity,
$-14.7 \to -10.6$ MMLU points (sealed-artifact arms at both sizes; the
served int8 arms give $-14.6$ and $-10.5$). The 4-bit baseline starts paying:
indistinguishable from FP16 at 4B ($-0.3$ points), it loses $3.1$ points
at 8B. And the quality gap between the two quantizations halves, from
14.5 to 7.5 points, while the 2-bit model overtakes AWQ on the memory
axes it lost at the 4B scale: 5.45~GB served versus 6.10, and a file
$1.9\times$ smaller. At 8B the choice between 4 and 2 bits is no
longer one-sided; it is 7.5 MMLU points against 11\% of VRAM and half the
disk. Data: \texttt{docs/data/echelle-4b-8b.csv}.

\textbf{Against the published 2-bit methods.} Tables~\ref{tab:campaign}
and~\ref{tab:campaign8b} compare against FP16 and against a deployed 4-bit
checkpoint. Table~\ref{tab:lit} adds the comparison a reader of the original
paper will make anyway. Rate is matched exactly rather than approximately:
every 2-bit row spends 48 bits per 24-weight block, and the configurations
differ only in how those bits split between direction index and gain
($48+0$, $47+1$ and $46+2$ in the original paper's Table~8). Ours is that
table's $47+1$ line, the one its own distortion study ranks first, while its
LLM results use the $48+0$ and $46+2$ variants. What our sealed file adds on
top of that payload --- full-precision tail columns and per-row scales,
about 8\% on the 4B and less on the 8B, whose \texttt{down\_proj} is exactly
block-aligned and carries no tail --- sits outside this comparison: the
original paper never states its own tail policy, so the two artifacts cannot
be set against each other at that level. The code rate can be, and it is
equal.

Absolute perplexities do not cross harnesses, so the ratio to each harness's
own FP16 baseline is the comparable quantity. The two baselines differ by
1.4\% on the 4B (12.41 against our 12.2369) and agree on the 8B to the two
decimals the original paper prints. The verdict is split. At 4B we sit level with the configurations nearest
ours: $\times1.38$ against $\times1.37$ for the 0-gain-bit variant and for
QTIP, behind the 2-gain-bit variant at $\times1.25$. At 8B we are behind both
LLVQ variants ($\times1.22$ against $\times1.13$, 0.78 perplexity points on a
shared baseline) and improve on QTIP alone. On MMLU, where only the 4B has
published points, the shortfall is wider: 55.59 against 60.7, on a baseline
our harness reproduces to 0.12 points.

We claim no statistical significance for these differences: the original
paper publishes no error bars, our $\pm$ is single-arm sampling error rather
than a paired test, and across papers the question samples differ. What the
matched payload does establish is that the 8B shortfall is not bought back by
a rate advantage, and that the excess our file carries on disk is smallest
exactly where the shortfall is largest. We do not explain it. The natural
suspect, roughly $100\times$ less calibration data, is bounded in
\S\ref{sec:limitations} at an effect too small to be the whole story --- and
that bound is measured on perplexity, which does not constrain MMLU.

\textbf{Cost of evidence.} Every cell above traces to a dated GPU job with
its billed cost (\texttt{docs/data/jobs.csv}). The full campaign behind
this paper (integration, ablations, negative results, both models) billed
\$22.83, of which \$12.61 is the 8B requantization and \$2.33 the kernel
campaign of \S\ref{sec:layouts}. Five short jobs from the CUDA porting day
are journaled without an amount, so that figure is a floor. The negative
result cannot be costed separately: both jobs that produced it (\$0.74 and \$0.77) timed
five or seven arms at once and are the source of Table~\ref{tab:layouts}
itself, so carrying \textsc{Golay70} through them cost cents. Adding an arm
to an existing run is nearly free.
