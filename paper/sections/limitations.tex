\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

We list these in the order a skeptical reader should weigh them.

\textbf{The MMLU deficit is real, reproduced, and unresolved.} $-14.6$
points against FP16 on the 4B served arm, reproduced three times across two
machines; on the sealed artifact, the arm every paired statistic uses, the
paired loss is 14.73 points, and it is 10.57 at 8B and 6.85 at 14B, all
three resolved. The per-subject profile shows the mechanism: abstract
algebra and accounting fall to 25\%, exactly chance, while history, law and
psychology hold above 80\%. Two-bit quantization damages \emph{reasoning}
far more than \emph{recall}, and a perplexity corpus mostly measures
recall; perplexity alone should never be presented as evidence of quality
at this rate. We measured and eliminated the obvious suspects rather than
leaving them open:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Output-side rotation.} The original paper's own ablation
puts its marginal effect near zero once input rotation is present.
\item \textbf{A free-magnitude variant with a closed-form scale solve}
(the configuration the original paper's appendix recommends, as we read
it): $\times1.99$ perplexity at full depth on the 0.6B proxy, killed by a
pre-registered gate.
\item \textbf{Calibration volume and corpus.} We calibrate on ${\sim}131$k
tokens against the original paper's 6\,100 sequences, roughly $100\times$
less, and it reports a $-9.5$-point drop without fine-tuning where we see
$-14.6$. A deliberate-contamination oracle (calibrating on the evaluation
text itself, an upper bound on anything volume, corpus or sequence length can
buy) recovers $1.6\%$ of perplexity on a proxy, $2.3\sigma$, closing 29\% of
that proxy's quantization gap; a $\times 13$ volume curve recovers $1.2\%$ at ${\sim}1.7\sigma$. A $\times 100$ calibration run is therefore
unlikely to be the answer, and we did not commission it.
\end{itemize} What remains
untested: reasoning-weighted calibration composition, learned per-column
scales (the original paper's fine-tuning, which recovers about two MMLU
points under its protocol), and low-rank compensation adapters. The bound on
the calibration item is weaker than it looks: it was measured on perplexity,
at 3 blocks, on a 0.6B proxy, and a perplexity oracle does not bound MMLU at
all.

\textbf{We do not measure QTIP.} Our motivation rests on the original
paper's report that its kernel is slower than QTIP's; we inherit that claim
rather than reproducing it. A 2-bit kernel comparison at matching shapes is
the single most valuable missing measurement in this paper --- the 4-bit arm
of Table~\ref{tab:layouts} is the same experiment against a different class
of competitor, and it is the one that changed our reading of our own result.
The same gap remains for Marlin's batched 4-bit GEMM and for the other 2-bit
vector-quantized systems of \S\ref{sec:related}.

\textbf{Two models end to end, one GPU, greedy decoding.} Everything
end-to-end is Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B on a single L40S with batch size 1 and
greedy decode. The third model, Qwen3-14B, was quantified, sealed and scored
but never served: it contributes quality to Table~\ref{tab:scale}, and a
b/param computed from its sealed bytes, but nothing measured on a card ---
no throughput, no on-card VRAM. (This paragraph read ``a \emph{quality} point
only \dots\ nothing about throughput or VRAM'' before the 2026-08-17
\texttt{rtbits} pass on that artifact; the byte-level memory figure exists
now, the card-level one still does not.) Batched
serving changes the arithmetic-intensity regime that makes
the kernel memory-bound; larger models change the embedding share and the
tail-column overhead.

\textbf{The scale argument is three points, and not a law.} On the 4B,
the summary of Table~\ref{tab:campaign} is that 4-bit AWQ dominates on
every axis except disk and served footprint, where our 5.162~b/param (the
exact-byte figure; 5.15 was the rounded card display) beats its 5.30 (5.302)
by about 3\%, a margin that does not, on its
own, justify a 14.6-point MMLU loss. The 8B moves every relevant number in
the 2-bit direction (Table~\ref{tab:campaign8b}): the quality gap narrows by
half and the memory verdict flips. The 14B keeps the direction and
\emph{loses the rate} (Table~\ref{tab:scale}): the excess
perplexity falls 42.8\% from 4B to 8B, $[-51.8, -33.5]$, and 13.9\% from 8B to
14B, $[-22.8, -4.9]$. Whether the
trend continues to the 70B class, the regime the sovereignty argument
actually cares about, is measurable with this methodology and is not measured
here --- and the third point is exactly the reason not to extrapolate from
the first two, as an earlier draft of this section did when it had only two.

Four qualifications on how that curve may be read. Every one of them moved on
2026-08-17, and each is recorded as changed rather than quietly rewritten.

First, the slowdown has \emph{two} verdicts, one per metric, and they must
never be merged into one sentence. On perplexity it is resolved: the 12
evaluation windows are the same text at every model size, so the two steps
can be differenced window by window rather than by composing standard errors,
and they differ by $-0.1010$ in log perplexity ratio (\emph{not} in the excess
parameterization the \texttt{fall} column uses), $[-0.1377, -0.0643]$,
$t = -6.06$,
with 11 of 12 windows agreeing in sign. On the MMLU gap it is not: no pairing
across sizes is available for a question set, so the test composes the two
campaigns' standard errors in quadrature, and that resolves the first step
($-6.96$ points, $p = 0.0001$) and not the second ($-1.40$, $p = 0.40$).
\emph{Neither result should be overclaimed and neither should be dropped.}
$p = 0.40$ is silence about that step, not evidence that the gap has stopped
closing; and the resolved perplexity knee says the first step fell harder than
the second, not where the curve flattens or that it reaches zero. The most
likely explanation of the divergence is power, not disagreement --- perplexity
averages the 49\,140 scored tokens of 12 paired windows where MMLU is a rate
over 2\,280 unpaired questions --- with two further differences that are real
and not disposable: the two metrics do not measure the same faculty, and the
perplexity knee is 2-bit against FP16 where the MMLU knee is 2-bit against the
4-bit baseline. That last one is checkable and does not carry the divergence
alone: against an AWQ reference the perplexity knee still resolves, though far
less comfortably ($-0.0576$, $[-0.1011, -0.0140]$, $t = -2.91$ against
$-6.06$), and that line should not be quoted with the confidence of the FP16
one. What follows operationally is what
this section already said --- no scaling law from three models --- now resting
on two tests that disagree about curvature rather than on caution alone. Both
are computed from published intervals and per-window logs, not separately
measured. This section previously said no test of a difference of differences
existed at all, and then, for a few hours, that the only test available
declined to resolve the slowdown --- a statement true of the MMLU gap, which
was then the only metric with bars at all three sizes, and that stopped being
the whole story the moment perplexity had them too. The first statement was
retired when the 14B dumps were recovered, the second when the 4B per-window
NLLs were.

Second, the third gap in the MMLU sequence (6.09 points) \emph{is} a paired
estimate, $[3.62, 8.52]$, McNemar $p = 1.1\times10^{-11}$. This section said
the opposite until 2026-08-17: that it was an unpaired difference of
micro-averages, that the 14B run's per-question dumps had never been
committed and were gone, and that it had no interval and must not be given
one. The dumps were not gone --- they were in the job's mounted output
bucket, and only the local copy had been looked for --- and the recomputation
cost no GPU time and did not move the point estimate.
See \S\ref{sec:evaluation}.

Third, the memory half of the argument now has all three points, not two.
This section said no whole-model b/param existed for the 14B because no
\texttt{rtbits} pass had been run on its sealed artifact; that pass has since
run, on the artifact recovered from the same bucket. We are below the AWQ
checkpoint at every size (5.162, 5.322, 5.106 against 5.302, 5.956, 5.404),
but \emph{the margin is not monotone} --- 2.6\%, 10.6\%, 5.5\% --- and it
should not be read as a tendency: it tracks the share of parameters the
embedding occupies, which AWQ leaves in FP16 and we quantize, and which peaks
at the 8B. What remains unmeasured above 8B is not memory but \emph{serving}:
the 14B was never run, so it has no throughput and no on-card VRAM figure.

Fourth, the perplexity half of that curve carried no error bars at all before
2026-08-17 and now carries them at all three sizes --- which is what made the
perplexity verdict above possible, and it arrived in two steps on the same
day. Six intervals came first, on the 8B and the 14B. The 4B's three were
declared unformable in the same journal, on the ground that its campaign log
had recorded aggregate perplexities and no per-window NLLs, with the cost of
restoring them budgeted as a re-run of two arms. They were in the job's log,
which the vendor had not purged, and they cost nothing. \emph{That is the
second output this project declared lost and then found somewhere it had not
looked, inside two days} --- the first being the 14B per-question dumps of the
qualification above, which were in a mounted output bucket. Both recoveries
changed a claim's evidential status without moving a point estimate, and both
had been preceded by a price for re-measuring rather than by a search. The
rule we now follow is to inventory a job's output bucket \emph{and} its logs
before pricing any re-run. The practice we do \emph{not} rely on is log
retention, which is neither documented nor guaranteed, which is why the
recovered output is committed to the repository rather than cited by job id.

\textbf{Our speedups are over FP16, not over the 4-bit state of the
practice.} Every $\times$ in \S\ref{sec:layouts} and
\S\ref{sec:integration} is against an FP16 baseline. At the \emph{kernel}
level the 4-bit comparison exists and goes against us: their GEMV reaches
$3.37\times$ the control where our production layout reaches $2.15\times$,
roughly a factor 1.6 --- a quotient of two medians from shared rounds rather
than a ratio formed round by round, so one significant figure. Their
\emph{engine} we have never run, so Tables~\ref{tab:campaign}
and~\ref{tab:campaign8b} carry no 4-bit throughput column and we claim
none.

\textbf{Speedups carry ranges, not third decimals.} Inter-process
dispersion on identical binaries spans several percent. Every
\emph{bench} ratio --- \S\ref{sec:layouts}, Table~\ref{tab:layouts},
Figure~\ref{fig:layouts} --- is a median of per-round ratios with a min--max
range, and effects smaller than the range are treated as unresolved. The
end-to-end ratios of \S\ref{sec:integration} are not of that kind and carry
no range: $2.03\times$, $1.12\times$ and $1.29\times$ are single-run
quotients of tok/s, with no rounds to take a median over. Two of the three
divide arms timed inside one job; the $1.29\times$ divides arms from two,
and is $1.30\times$ when formed within the job that ran both. Their second
decimal should be read as dispersion, not as measurement.

\textbf{The rotated basis is a real serving constraint.} Weights are stored
in the incoherence-rotated basis; any consumer of the artifact must apply
the activation rotation. Our rotation kernel is verified bit-exact on CUDA
but exists nowhere else (no Metal port yet), so the served path is currently
NVIDIA-only, which sits awkwardly with the sovereignty motivation and is
first in line for future work.
