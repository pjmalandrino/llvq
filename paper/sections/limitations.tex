\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

We list these in the order a skeptical reader should weigh them.

\textbf{The MMLU deficit is real, reproduced, and its leading suspects are
eliminated rather than confirmed.} $-14.6$ points against FP16, reproduced
three times across two machines. The per-subject profile shows the
mechanism: abstract algebra and accounting fall to 25\% --- exactly chance
--- while history, law and psychology hold above 80\%. Two-bit quantization
damages \emph{reasoning} far more than \emph{recall}, and a perplexity
corpus mostly measures recall; perplexity alone should never be presented as
evidence of quality at this rate.

The obvious suspect is calibration volume: we use ${\sim}131$k tokens against
the original paper's 6\,100 sequences, roughly $100\times$ less, and they
report a $-9.5$-point drop where we see $-14.6$. We tested the ceiling of
that family instead of paying for the run. Calibrating on the evaluation
corpus itself --- deliberate contamination, an upper bound on anything
volume, corpus or sequence length can buy --- recovers $1.6\%$ of perplexity
($2.3\sigma$, closing 29\% of the quantization gap), and an honest
$\times 13$ volume curve recovers $1.2\%$. A $\times 100$ calibration run is
therefore not the answer, and we did not commission it.

Two caveats on that bound, which is why the deficit is open rather than
closed: it was measured on perplexity, at 3 blocks, on a 0.6B model, and a
perplexity oracle does not bound MMLU at all. Calibration \emph{composition}
--- reasoning-heavy data, which targets the failing subjects specifically ---
is untested and is the surviving suspect. We prefer to state that the cause
is unknown than to name a suspect our own measurements have bounded.

\textbf{We do not measure QTIP.} Our motivation rests on the original
paper's report that its kernel is slower than QTIP's; we inherit that claim
rather than reproducing it, and no QTIP arm ran on our machine. A kernel
comparison at matching matvec shapes is the single most valuable missing
measurement in this paper. The same holds, less centrally, for Marlin at
4 bits and for the other 2-bit vector-quantized systems cited in
\S\ref{sec:related}.

\textbf{One model, one GPU, greedy decoding.} Everything end-to-end is
Qwen3-4B on a single L40S with batch size 1 and greedy decode. Batched
serving changes the arithmetic-intensity regime that makes bytes read the
dominant term at all --- and \S\ref{sec:attribution} shows the kernel is
already only two thirds of the way to its own bandwidth floor at batch 1;
larger models change the embedding share and the tail-column overhead. We measured one scale point (8B, quality only), not a scaling
law.

\textbf{The scale argument is a bet, not a result.} On the 4B itself,
Table~\ref{tab:campaign} says 4-bit AWQ dominates on every axis except disk,
and our served footprint beats its 5.30~b/param by 3\% --- a margin that
does not, on its own, justify a 14.6-point MMLU loss. The case for the 2-bit
point is the model class that does not otherwise fit, and that case rests on
the single 8B degradation point and on extrapolation.

\textbf{AWQ speed is not comparable.} The AWQ arm was never executed in our
engine (its quality was, under matching fingerprints). Cross-engine
throughput comparisons are meaningless and we make none.

\textbf{Speedups carry ranges, not third decimals.} Inter-process
dispersion on identical binaries spans several percent; all ratios are
medians of per-round ratios with min--max ranges, and effects smaller than
the range are treated as unresolved.

\textbf{The rotated basis is a real serving constraint.} Weights are stored
in the incoherence-rotated basis; any consumer of the artifact must apply
the activation rotation. Our rotation kernel is verified bit-exact on CUDA
but exists nowhere else (no Metal port yet), so the served path is currently
NVIDIA-only, which sits awkwardly with the sovereignty motivation and is
first in line for future work.
