\section{The Fused Multi-Shell Decoder}
\label{sec:decoder}

\subsection{Background: what a $\Lambda_{24}$ index is}

LLVQ quantizes weights in blocks of 24. Each block is normalized and its
direction snapped to the nearest point of the Leech lattice $\Lambda_{24}$,
restricted to the ball $\Lambda_{24}(12)$ (all points of squared norm
$\le 2\cdot 12$ in the standard scaling). The ball contains
$70\,486\,236\,999\,360$ points, so a direction index costs
$\lceil\log_2\cdot\rceil = 47$ bits; one gain bit selects between two
per-tensor magnitude centroids, for 48 bits per 24-weight block --- exactly
2 bits per weight of payload.

In the integer coordinate system $\sqrt{8}\,\Lambda_{24}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{24}$,
lattice points are characterized by three simultaneous conditions tied to the
binary Golay code $\mathcal{G}_{24}$ \citep{conway1999sphere}: a parity
condition (all coordinates even, or all odd), a Golay condition (the support
of coordinates in a fixed residue class mod~4 must be a codeword), and a sum
condition mod~8. The practical consequence is that every lattice point in
the ball belongs to one of 301 \emph{classes} --- orbits under coordinate
permutation and admissible sign changes --- each defined by a Golay codeword
weight, a multiset of coordinate magnitudes, and a coset (even/odd). The
index format enumerates classes and, within a class, arrangements and signs,
in a fixed order; the resulting 48-bit-per-block format~v1 is bijective and
pinned by tests down to its mixed-radix composition orders.

\subsection{The encoder/decoder asymmetry}

Everything about making this practical follows from one asymmetry: the
\emph{encoder} (nearest-neighbor search over $7\times10^{13}$ points) runs
offline, once per model, and may cost hours; the \emph{decoder}
(index~$\to$~vector) runs inside every matvec and must cost shifts and
masks. The original paper's kernel sidesteps the problem by decoding a
single shell ($m=3$); at the 2-bit rate the codebook spans 12 shells and
301 classes, and a naive per-class branch would diverge across every warp.

Our kernel never decodes the 47-bit index at inference time at all. An
offline \emph{transcoder} expands each block index once, at load time, into
a decoded-field representation that the kernel reads directly
(\S\ref{sec:layouts}); in its simplest form (\textsc{Slot32}) the fields are
a 9-bit class identifier, the gain bit, a 24-bit sign mask, and four 24-bit
coordinate masks selecting which coordinates carry each magnitude level.
Reconstruction of coordinate $i$ is then a table lookup of the class's level
values, a mask test per level, and a sign flip --- identical instruction
sequences for every lane regardless of class, hence zero warp divergence.
The per-class tables (level values, shell norms, arrangement counts) total a
few kilobytes and live in constant memory. Because every point of a given
shell has the same norm, the normalization factor between the lattice
integer coordinates and the unit direction is a per-class constant folded
into the same table; the union-of-shells rescaling that the original paper's
Appendix~G flags as the hardware cost of the multi-shell codebook reduces to
one multiply per block.

\subsection{Verification discipline}

Lattice decoders fail silently: a wrong sign convention or a swapped
enumeration order produces plausible-looking weights and a quietly ruined
model. Three layers of defense are in place, and we consider them as much a
contribution as the kernel:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Bit-exact transcoding.} The transcoder that expands format~v1
into each of the five VRAM layouts of \S\ref{sec:layouts} is verified
bit-exact across all layouts, and its test suite has been hardened by
mutation testing (${\sim}25$ mutants killed). A mutant that survives means
either a weak test or dead code; both stop the line.

\item \textbf{Line-by-line numerical verification before any timing.} Every
benchmark first checks every output row of every arm against an independent
f64 reference --- $1\,105\,920$ rows for the full-model benchmark --- with
worst-case errors of $2.2$--$3.0\times10^{-8}$, consistent with f32
accumulation. No arm is timed until it is proven to compute the right thing.

\item \textbf{Invariant locks upstream.} The lattice code itself is pinned
by exact combinatorial invariants --- the kissing number $196\,560$, the
theta-series coefficients, and the exact class-cardinality sum
$N(13)=280\,974\,212\,784\,720$ --- which no incorrect enumeration constraint
can reproduce.
\end{itemize}
