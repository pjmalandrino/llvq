\begin{abstract}
Leech-lattice vector quantization gives the best published quality at 2 bits,
but its kernel decodes a single lattice shell, and the multi-shell decoder
the rate actually requires was never written. We write it: a fused
dequantize-plus-matvec CUDA kernel for the full 301-class codebook, and a
family of VRAM layouts that decouple the on-disk rate from the rate the
kernel reads. Replacing one-hot coordinate masks with binary bit planes makes
the format simultaneously smaller and faster --- 4.80 against 5.51 bits per
weight, $2.15\times$ an FP16 matvec --- and a sparse overlay reaches 4.34 at
bit-exact reconstruction. Below that the curve turns: a Golay-payload layout
reaches 3.59 bits per weight, but its per-slot coset decode leaves the kernel
compute-bound, and hoisting that decode to block level, at zero change to the
stored bytes, recovers only part of the loss. Both attempts missed acceptance
criteria fixed before measuring.

We also run the comparison this literature usually omits. A deployed 4-bit
GEMV, ported unchanged into the same process and the same rounds, converts
88\% of its byte advantage into time where our best layout converts 65\%.
That gap, not the speedup against FP16, is the result: what is left to win
lies in the decode, not in the format. Integrated end to end, a 2-bit
Qwen3-4B serves at 88.4~tok/s in 2.60~GB of VRAM; with the output head held
identical in both arms (our dense arm materialized 778~MB per token;
\S\ref{sec:integration} has the fix), the kernel-plus-format gain is
$1.12\times$ on the 4B and $1.29\times$ on the 8B; no such same-head arm was
run at 14B, which serves at 42.9~tok/s in 9.39~GB against 17.0 and 29.54 for
its dense arm, once an opt-in lifted the rotation kernel's shared-memory
ceiling. That ceiling, now measured at 101\,376 bytes, is where the served
path stops: the rotation is a Walsh--Hadamard, so one block holds the whole
activation, and the 32B class overruns it by 1\,024 bytes. Two bits costs $\times1.384$ perplexity
and 14.6 MMLU points against FP16 at 4B (the served int8 arm; 14.73 on
the sealed artifact). Scale helps: across 4B, 8B and 14B the excess perplexity
over FP16 falls by 42.8\% and then by 13.9\%, while the MMLU gap to a 4-bit
baseline goes 14.45, 7.49, 6.09 points --- all three subject-stratified paired
bootstrap estimates with 95\% intervals, the last of which an earlier draft
printed as an unpaired difference until that run's per-question dumps were
recovered. Whether the closing itself slows has two answers and we report
both, each with its metric attached: paired window by window, the perplexity
slowdown is resolved (the two steps differ by $-0.1010$ in log perplexity
ratio, $[-0.1377, -0.0643]$, $t = -6.06$), while on the MMLU gap only the first step
separates from zero ($-6.96$ points, $p = 0.0001$, against $-1.40$ and
$p = 0.40$). The divergence is most likely power --- perplexity averages
49\,140 paired tokens where MMLU rates 2\,280 unpaired questions --- and a $p$
of 0.40 is silence rather than constancy. Three points, no law. On memory the
served model is below that 4-bit baseline at all three sizes --- 5.162, 5.322
and 5.106 b/param against 5.302, 5.956 and 5.404 --- by a margin that is not
monotone, since it tracks the embedding share and not the method; at 8B that
is 5.45~GB against 6.10 from a file half the size. The ledger bills \$58.80 of
recorded GPU time, \$55.59 of it behind the tables and \$1.48 more behind the
14B serving figures and that ceiling.
\end{abstract}
